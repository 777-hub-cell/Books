## 1. The woman in the millrace [start]

The boy had tied her wrist to a goose.

It was a large grey goose, standing in the shallows and objecting to everything. When she moved, the string pulled tight. The boy looked up from the bank.

“Good. You're not dead.”

She sat in six inches of water. A millwheel turned behind her, bringing down a regular spill of foam. Her left shoe was missing. She could name the wheel, the goose, the dark stain spreading from her torn sleeve. She could not think of a single place she had been before this one.

“Why did you tie me to that?”

“So I could hear if you floated off.”

He was perhaps nine, with a sack apron and a split in one eyebrow. He unwound the string and offered a stick. She took it. Getting out hurt in several ordinary, reassuring ways.

On the bank, he gave her a piece of cold oatcake. She knew how to chew. She knew the bird ought not to be fed her last crumbs. Somewhere inside her were instructions for living, neatly arranged around an empty space.

“I'm Pip,” he said. “What's yours?”

She tried. Water dripped from her chin. The boy waited longer than most people would have.

“You can tell me tomorrow. My aunt writes hers on the door.”

Above the mill stood a town of red roofs and narrow yards. Beyond it, fields climbed towards a wooded rim. A road ran into the trees. A man was walking up it, carrying a blue bowl with both hands.

Pip dropped the string.

“Master Elian!”

The man did not turn. Between the trees, three white stones marked the sides of the road. He was nearly at them.

## 1. Before the white stones [rescue]

She caught the man by the belt. He spun and struck her with the bowl, hard enough to chip a tooth. She held on. His feet scraped backwards through wet leaves.

“My wife,” he said. “She's waiting.”

Pip came panting up beside them. “Your wife is at the pottery.”

“I must take this to her.”

“She's at the pottery.”

The man looked from Pip to the bowl. Inside it was a spoon and a folded scrap of cloth. Beyond the stones, the road continued through harmless-looking trees. The woman wanted to let him go. He was frightened of her, and she had no authority to hold him.

Then Pip said, very quietly, “Three steps. That's all you get.”

She kept her hand on the belt.

A woman in a leather apron arrived with two neighbours. Nobody scolded Elian. His wife put her hand inside his and said they needed to inspect the kiln. They walked down together, carrying the bowl between them.

The stranger's bleeding hand had left a print on his coat. She put it in her mouth.

“He'll hate that,” Pip said. “He likes his good coat.”

After a moment she laughed, although it hurt. Pip did not. He was looking at her feet, checking where they had stopped.

## 1. What the road takes [observe]

She went after Pip, but the cut in her leg opened when she tried to run. By the time she reached the trees, the man had passed the white stones.

He took one step onto the road beyond them. His blue bowl tilted. On the second step, his knees bent as though he were about to sit down.

Pip caught her sleeve before she reached him.

“Don't go after.”

The man made a third small, shuffling step. He fell sideways. The bowl rolled back towards the stones, whole and empty.

She waited for him to move. She had seen a body fall before. That much her own stomach told her.

Neighbours came with a long cart rope. A woman in a leather apron took one look and went to her knees, but she did not cross. They hooked the man's belt from inside the stones and pulled him back. His wife covered his face with her apron.

“He was taking me breakfast,” she said. “I was only at the kiln.”

Nobody answered. Pip stood by the bowl. The stranger picked it up for him, and he stepped away from her.

On the inner face of the nearest stone, a warning had been cut and recut until the letters resembled a wound: FIRST THE WAY. SECOND THE VOICE. THIRD THE BREATH.

## 2. Things written on doors [kitchen]

Pip's aunt lived over a repair shop. Her door said SERA VALE, SEAMSTRESS in black paint. Underneath, in smaller writing: PIP LIVES HERE. HE HAS PERMISSION TO EAT.

Sera read the second line before she opened the door for him.

“You missed breakfast.”

“I found someone.”

“I can see that.”

She gave the stranger a stool, a dry shift and a basin. Her hands were broad, the nails cut short. She set stitches in the torn calf without asking questions that could not yet be answered. The woman watched the needle go in and out. Once she flinched; Sera waited and began again.

“What do we call you?”

“I don't know.”

“We can leave the box empty.”

She had drawn a small household chart on the wall. Pip's face was sketched beside a picture of a goose. The space below it was blank.

Pip fetched a nub of charcoal. “You came out of the water. Reed?”

The name did not bring anything back. That made it possible to accept.

“Reed,” she said.

Sera wrote it down. “This isn't a debt. Bed's in the loft. If you're staying, you can help me turn mattresses when that leg closes.”

She put a bowl of barley soup on the table and turned to the stove. Pip tipped his portion of onion into Reed's bowl while his aunt's back was turned. Sera, without looking round, told him to put half of it back.

For the first time since waking, Reed was able to think about something as small as eating.

After supper Pip pushed two objects towards her: Sera's spare pocketbook and a little bird he had cut from willow, badly enough that it could also have been a boot.

“The book helps you know things,” he said. “The bird's just mine.”

## 2. The blank box [notebook]

Reed took the pocketbook. The first pages listed needle sizes and names beside amounts of flour. She asked before using the empty leaves.

Sera gave her a pencil. “Date it. We use the mill tally, not the council calendar. Oren changes that when he corrects the festivals.”

Pip put the wooden bird away.

At Reed's request he drew the town: a crooked cluster of houses, a river, the hill where the old hall stood. He added the forest and the dairy meadows. When she asked how far the boundary extended, he used both hands to measure a distance on the table.

“All round the bowl of the hills. Whole day's walk to the far sheep.”

“Who lives outside?”

“Nobody who sends letters.”

Sera set a cup down. “We know there were travellers. There are coins from elsewhere. Nobody's arrived in my lifetime.”

“Until me.”

“You came from the upper millrace. That comes from inside.”

Reed wrote it down. She had been imagining a road home. Now she drew a line under the possibility that home was here.

## 2. A bird with no beak [bird]

Reed took the bird. Pip showed her the black groove beneath one wing, where the knife had slipped and cut his finger.

“It looks down because it's thinking,” he said.

“What about?”

“Things to eat.”

They arranged a greeting. He would ask whether the bird had flown. She would say it had eaten too much. Pip tried the exchange twice, correcting her on the second attempt because he wanted exactly the same words.

Sera listened while she darned. At last she said, “Tell her what a greeting can do.”

“Help,” Pip said.

“And what it can't.”

He studied a loose shaving in his palm. “Make you remember.”

Reed held the bird by its thick wing. “Then we'll try it anyway.”

Sera tied a loop of thread through it so Reed could wear it under her shift. The needle caught on a hard ridge beneath Reed's collarbone. A pale, old scar ran there: three little punctures in a curve.

Sera touched none of them. “Someone sewed this in a hurry.”

Reed lifted her hand to the marks. For a moment she smelled hot wool. Then it went away, leaving the kitchen and Pip pretending not to watch her.

## 3. Fenmere [market]

Fenmere held a market every fourth morning. Reed learned this by carrying Sera's mending there, past strips of drying flax and a yard full of turnip peelings for the pigs. The town smelled of wet leather, yeast and river mud.

At the grain stall, two women checked a balance against a stone kept in a sealed box. A third read the tally aloud. When one forgot a number, they began again without changing the agreed price.

“It takes ages,” Pip complained.

“It takes less time than fighting,” Sera said.

Work was taught in pairs. The old potter watched his apprentice's hands; a brewer followed a board covered in pictures. People could lose the afternoon when they first made bread and still make bread. They could know that a woman was their daughter because a register said so, yet move aside when she tried to kiss them.

At a stall selling buttons, Reed saw it happen. The daughter bought a green one and left it on her mother's table. Neither cried until they were out of the other's sight.

Every house had names. The bridge had a warning to count the children both ways. At the well, someone had hung a list of people who must not draw water alone.

Fenmere had orchards, three mills, a small ironworking hearth fed from the western bog, and salt made from a bitter spring. Its roofs needed repair. Its sheep had to be shorn. The curse did not do any of the work.

Across the square, a bell began to ring. Sera stopped counting buttons.

It rang once, then again. Everyone listened for a third stroke. It did not come.

“That's tomorrow's bell,” Sera said.

## 3. An apprentice's hands [mill-work]

Before they went to ask questions, Sera sent Reed to the lower mill with a bundle of repaired sacks. Pip went with her. His goose was already there, waiting for the miller to leave his lunch unattended.

“You said he wasn't a pet.”

“He isn't. He doesn't like me.”

Boots followed them through the gate, hissing at Reed's bare ankle.

Inside, a girl was chalking the hopper doors. She had written OPEN over a closed one, rubbed it away and written CHECK WITH TOM. Tom, the miller, waited beside her.

“Do I have to do this every time?” the girl asked.

“Until you know which day it is.”

“I know which day it is.”

“Then why did you put yesterday's flour with today's?”

The girl put down the chalk. Reed expected an argument. Instead Tom opened both bins and checked them with her. They sifted a little onto the same slate and compared the dampness. When they finished, the girl corrected the sack labels herself.

“She lost the week she started here,” Tom told Reed as she untied Sera's bundle. “Can still dress the stones better than I can.”

“I can hear you,” the girl said.

“I meant you to.”

Reed asked why they could not simply use dates from the council. Tom showed her a long beam notched near the ceiling. Each notch was a winter when the river had frozen hard enough to close the wheel. Beneath it was another, finer set of marks, made by generations of millers recording the months between.

“We don't take somebody's word for a count,” he said. “Not even ours.”

The roof beams were older than the apprentice's great-grandmother, whose name Tom read from a page. They had been replaced one at a time. The wheel outside was new; it had been new eleven times, according to the maintenance record. No single person had remembered making all of it, and it had kept turning.

Reed looked at her own hands. She had expected a lost life to be wholly lost. It was frightening to discover how much a person could do while knowing so little about herself.

Tom gave her a barley token for carrying the sacks. He asked her to tell Sera that she had charged too little for the heavy stitching. Pip immediately asked whether that meant she should charge more next time.

“It means you should let a man say thank you without pricing it,” Tom said.

Pip considered that answer all the way out of the yard.

## 3. The keeper of dates [archive]

The public record room smelled of tallow. Reed found an old woman seated at its only sunny window, slicing spoiled edges from account books. A cloth on her knee caught the crumbs.

“Marta,” she said. “If you've come for a birth, give me a year.”

Reed explained that she needed her own.

Marta looked at her properly. “That will be difficult.”

They worked backwards from clothing. Reed's ruined dress had hand-worked buttonholes and expensive thread. Marta found three illustrations of similar sleeves, all in books older than Sera. She did not announce a conclusion. She fetched another volume.

On its first page was a count of households before the enclosure: millers, charcoal burners, shepherds, refugees in the north pasture. The date was one hundred and ninety-seven years before the current mill tally.

Someone had scraped a signature from the bottom. Three pinholes remained where a seal had been fastened.

“Why ruin that part?” Reed asked.

“I don't know. I wrote a note about it.” Marta searched her own pockets and produced a dozen slips. “Somewhere.”

Reed found the note tucked in the binding. It said CHECK THE WATER COPY. NORTH BRIDGE. Beneath it, more recently: DO NOT ASK OREN FIRST.

Marta read it twice. “I dislike him less on mornings when I can't remember why.”

She lent Reed a rubbing of the damaged page. The copied pinholes made Reed touch her collarbone.

## 4. The boundary by water [perimeter]

Reed walked to the river fence before she went to the granary. A strip of wool had caught on it. Beyond, the river passed between two white stones set on opposite banks, then dropped into a gorge.

A ferryman was repairing the fence. His right foot was missing. He used the bottom of a broken oar as a crutch.

“If you're thinking boats are different, they're not.”

She told him she was thinking about water. The current was leaving. The fish were leaving. A reed stem snapped off upstream could leave.

“Anything but us,” he said. “The third advance takes you, however you're carried. Horse, boat, somebody's back. The boundary counts the motion. We've written it down.”

He showed her a small box fastened inside the fence. It contained copies of accounts. None were tests performed for her benefit. She read of a cart pulled back after its second wheel-turn; of a woman who had been carried out unconscious and died when the men carrying her made their third advance. The exact measure changed with the movement. The warning did not.

“I'm not going to try it,” she said.

“People say that before they come back with an idea.”

“Your foot?”

“Mill accident. Not everything's the curse.”

Reed apologised. He nodded as if she had paid a small, appropriate toll, and went back to the fence.

She stood watching water go where she could not. Her first thought was that someone ought to have found a way. Her second was of the accounts in the box, each an attempt made by a person who had also believed that thought was new.

On the path back she found the remains of an old signpost. Its arms had been turned to point inward. Beneath a layer of moss she could make out the words RETURN TO THE REFUGE. The carving showed the same rooted figure that appeared on the oldest public warning stones.

She made a sketch of it on the wrapping from Sera's sacks. She was beginning to have things she did not want to lose.

## 4. The bell brought forward [council]

Oren met Reed outside the granary. He wore an ordinary coat with one fine silver clasp. His clerk carried a slate full of erased white smudges.

“You'll need a ration mark,” he said. “Sera shouldn't have to feed another adult from her allowance.”

She had expected a threat. He gave her three barley tokens instead.

“Why is the bell early?”

Oren sent the clerk to count the granary locks. When they were alone, he rubbed a raw patch at the base of his thumb.

“The thorn root under the hall was pushing through the sluice. I had it cut back. The water's safe. The bell has been irregular since.”

“What does it take?”

“A memory. At each new moon. A little more if the enclosure is damaged. First the distant things. Eventually the near ones. We can't make it take a chosen memory. We can keep one present by speaking it through the bell, but then we leave the rest exposed.”

“And you don't think people should know you damaged it?”

“I think they should keep eating while I find out how to repair it.”

Children were chasing a hoop round the well. He waited until they passed.

“There are people who'll burn down the hall if they think someone can be blamed. Our winter grain is beneath it. Tell them what you know. Decide whether you know enough first.”

He was afraid, Reed thought. He also wanted her to carry the fear in his place.

## 4. In the open square [warn]

Reed climbed onto the well curb and told the market what Oren had said. She repeated his admission exactly, including the part about the sluice.

Hessa came first. Others stopped work. Someone shouted that the granary should be seized. Sera put herself between the well and a man carrying a mallet.

“We want witnesses,” Reed said. “People to check what happens. People to keep copies.”

It took a long time. Nobody wanted the same thing. By the end, each craft had chosen someone to attend the hall. They agreed that stores would be counted before anyone touched a lock.

Oren stayed at the edge of the crowd. When Reed stepped down, he took back the unopened barley tokens.

“You'll be fed at the public table,” he said. “The public will want to know what they're buying.”

Sera walked home with her. “He's right about that part.”

Pip had collected seven different versions of Reed's speech. They spent the evening correcting them. Underneath all seven was the same small error: everyone remembered that somebody else had rung the bell.

Reed agreed to wait until morning before speaking publicly. In return, she asked for access to the hall beneath the granary.

## 5. The fourth bowl [night-watch]

That night, before supper, Sera asked Reed to help turn the mattress in the loft. Reed said her leg was well enough. Sera said that was not what she had asked.

They did it slowly, stopping on the middle stair. The mattress smelled of sun-dried rushes. Reed had to squeeze past Sera to keep it from folding into the doorway. It was hard work, and she was pleased with herself afterwards for having done it.

In the loft Sera found an old ribbon under the bed. She put it in her apron pocket without looking at it properly.

“Was it yours?” Reed asked.

Sera brought out a small square of linen. On it she had stitched a name, LENN, and a list of dates. “My husband's. I have the marriage record. I have the day he died. I can't always find him between.”

“Do the dates help?”

“They stop other people telling me it didn't happen.”

She put the linen back. Reed could hear Pip downstairs repeating the sums he had been set. He got to seven threes and went back to six.

“Sometimes I want somebody to tell me,” Sera said. “It seems wasteful to keep a bed for a man whose face I can't remember. Then I hear myself thinking that and I put the extra bowl away again.”

Reed waited. Sera looked embarrassed at having said so much. She took the mattress from Reed's hands and gave her a pillow instead.

“Shake that out. Then we can eat.”

Downstairs, Pip had got to eight threes. He asked Reed what came next. She knew at once. He asked when she had learned it. She did not know.

“That's all right,” he said, after thinking. “You can teach me now.”

They did nine threes together while Sera set the table.

## 5. Tomorrow's portion [supper]

At supper Sera served three bowls and put away a fourth. Reed watched her do it. So did Pip.

“Who is that for?” Reed asked.

“Nobody.” Sera kept her hand on the cupboard door. “There used to be someone. I don't want the rest of you thinking I haven't enough.”

Pip stirred his soup until the barley settled. He asked whether Reed would still be there after the bell.

She wanted to promise. Instead she asked Sera for a second piece of paper. They wrote where Reed had been found, what Sera had done for her, and the things Pip wanted recorded about himself. He insisted that his goose was a gander, named Boots, and not his pet. He minded it for the miller.

“And I want to build a boat,” he said.

“There's a ferry already.”

“It's a bad boat.”

They drew a better one. Pip tried a greeting: “Has the bird flown?” Reed answered, “It ate too much.” He asked if they could use it tomorrow. She said yes. Sera mended in the doorway where the light lasted longest. Reed could see how a person might begin a life here and, little by little, lose track of having wanted another.

Before bed, Pip said his mother's name was Ada. She had died of a fever two winters ago. He could still remember the scar on her elbow and the way she tested porridge with her smallest finger.

“It's nearly gone,” he said. “When I try, it's like knowing where a tooth was.”

Outside, someone began covering the bell's sounding holes with wool. It would not stop the tithe. It made the sound easier for the children.

## 5. The next morning [keep-pip]

At the bell, Reed repeated the words she and Pip had agreed to use for a greeting. She had rehearsed the most recent, most fragile thing she owned. The bell passed over a living memory while it was being spoken. Everything else lay exposed. It did not protect everything.

The next morning she recognised Pip before he spoke. But when she went to ask Sera for the pocketbook, she could not remember its colour, or where Sera had left it, or why the notes inside mattered.

Reading restored the facts. It did not restore the evening in which she had learned them.

Pip tried the greeting. Reed answered correctly. His relief made her ashamed of how glad she was to have kept him at another part of her mind's expense.

## 5. A familiar stranger [keep-clue]

At the bell, Reed pressed the copied evidence under her palm and rehearsed it: three holes, the crown, the witness, words beneath the bridge. She woke remembering why each mattered.

She kept reading until the bell fell silent. The words stayed present while it rang. The person she had come to know over them did not.

A boy was sitting beside the bed. She pulled the blanket to her throat.

“Who let you in?”

He looked at the floor. “I live here.”

She found his face on the household chart. Pip. She knew the name when she read it. The certainty of his place in her life had disappeared.

He asked a question about a bird. She waited, trying to help by concentrating. At last he said it had eaten too much and climbed down the ladder.

Sera let Reed read the pocketbook alone. Later, Pip agreed to show her the north bridge, provided she stopped saying she was sorry every time he handed her something.

“I know you didn't pick me to go,” he said.

Reed could not honestly say that. She told him she had chosen to hold the clues.

He listened, then moved his chair a little farther from hers. The distance stayed there all morning.

## 6. The people who still came [after-bell]

After the bell, the repair shop opened late. Customers stood outside with bundles in their arms. Sera let them in three at a time and made each one check the name on the ticket before handing anything over.

One man denied that a child's coat was his. It had six different patches and a sleeve turned twice. Sera showed him the payment record. He read it, looked at the coat again and asked whether he had a child.

Pip went upstairs. Reed stayed behind the counter because Sera had asked her to mark collections. She wanted to put down the pen. Instead she wrote the man's name while he and Sera looked for a household witness.

“He's at his sister's,” the witness said when she arrived. “You arranged it. Your son. His name's Benn.”

“Why did I send him away?”

“So he wouldn't watch the bell take you.”

They sat on the steps together until the man was ready to go. He held the coat in his lap as carefully as if the child were inside it.

Reed waited until the shop was empty before asking whether this happened every month.

“Not to everyone,” Sera said. “That's how we keep going. Some of us can still tell the others.”

Outside, a woman began singing a list of grain measures. A second voice joined, then stopped when they reached different numbers. Reed could hear them checking the verse against a board at the market. These were not the empty people she had begun to fear becoming. They were busy, impatient people who had work to do and kept finding new ways to do it.

She went upstairs. Pip was carving the rib of a boat from a split branch. He moved the knife away when she sat down. Reed asked whether he wanted company or quiet.

“Company,” he said. “Quiet company.”

She sat until he asked her to hold the wood. They did not mention the greeting. When Sera called them to the bridge, Pip put the unfinished rib on the sill where the sun could dry it.

## 6. The water copy [bridge]

Hessa lent them the pottery punt. Pip said it leaked on the wrong side. Reed asked what the right side would be. He considered refusing to answer, then showed her where to put her weight.

The north bridge was older than the houses around it. Its inner abutment had been built from carved blocks turned face down. Water slid over half an inscription. Sera held the punt while Reed worked beneath the arch with a lamp.

Three figures had been cut into the stone: a woman bearing a circlet, a crowd holding a roll, and a narrow person with roots instead of feet. Beneath them ran a line of text. Reed read aloud what she could reach.

“Until the danger passes, keep us. Let the Crown hold our pledge. Let the Witness hold our names.”

Around the three figures ran a smaller sentence: OUR WAY, OUR VOICE, OUR BREATH, UNTIL THE KEEPING ENDS. Reed traced the words back to the first figure. The same three things were written on the boundary stones.

The remaining lines lay underwater. With a blade, Reed scraped mineral deposits from the edge. She saw a word repeated on both sides of a long crack: RETURN.

Above, a cart crossed the bridge. Dust sifted down. The punt shifted and the lamp touched the water, hissing out.

Sera pulled them into daylight. A piece of the abutment came away behind them. The rest began to tilt. Pip was still crouched in the bow, holding the borrowed lamp and a fistful of wet paper.

## 6. The handhold [save-boy]

Reed went into the river. She caught the back of Pip's apron and pushed him towards Sera. The bow lifted, spilling the paper. A stone slab dropped into the place where his legs had been.

Sera dragged him onto the bank. He vomited water, then began arguing that he had not needed to be carried. Reed sat beside him until he ran out of breath.

## 6. What she held [save-stone]

Reed knew what she had done because she had made herself write it. She held the statement up. Her hand was bleeding. She had to read twice before she understood why the woman was weeping.

The Keeper had fallen in pieces among the grain bins. They were pieces of root. Nobody could say when it had stopped having a face.

Outside, someone shouted that the thorns along the road were dying. Pip looked towards the door, then back at Sera. The seamstress took out the household chart. She held it where they could all see.

“Start here,” she said.

## 11. The first provision [winter-book]

Before she slept, Reed made the assembly say where the extra grain would come from if the doubled tithe left more people unable to work alone. Oren brought the reserve accounts to the chamber door.

“We have enough for winter if the western fields are sown,” he said.

Sera asked who would sow them. Oren began to name the usual households, stopped and read the maintenance plan instead. Two of the named workers were on the well list. One had forgotten which children lived in his house.

They changed the plan. Apprentices would work in larger groups. Older people who could still teach would move nearer the fields. The public table would keep open through the winter. None of it removed the extra taking. It arranged who would carry it.

Reed watched them make the arrangements from the stone bed. She had asked for time; here was the price of having some.

“Don't write that I saved you,” she said.

Marta had been recording the meeting. She looked up, then drew a line through a heading and began another.

Oren set the accounts on the floor. Without the silver clasp, his coat looked much older. He asked if there was anything she wanted taken upstairs.

Reed gave him the public release copy. She had kept the page beside her in case she changed her mind. He recognised what he was being asked to carry and did not reach for it at once.

“Give it to three people,” she said. “Don't choose them yourself.”

He took it to the door. Sera stood aside to let him pass, watching until he reached the light.

Later, when the sleep had begun to settle and Reed could no longer tell whether her eyes were open, she heard Marta reading the revised heading. TERMS OF THIS RENEWAL. Below it she put the date, the names of the witnesses and the warning about the doubled tithe. She read all of it aloud before closing the book.

## 11. Another year [renew]

Reed asked for the renewal to be read. The Keeper did not make it sound merciful. The cut root must grow again. Until it did, every new moon would collect twice the old tithe. The guarantor would sleep, and could wake only when the keeping was called to account again.

“Write the release terms in public,” Reed told the assembly. “All of them. Keep the roll outside the vault.”

Sera wrote. Marta made a second copy. It was a better renewal than the last one. Reed could see how a person might use that fact to bear signing it.

Pip did not say goodbye. He took his boat drawing off the table and folded it until it fitted in his pocket.

In the chamber below the sluice, Reed lay on the stone bed. Sera covered her with a blanket that smelled of the repair shop.

“I can't promise I'll be here,” Sera said.

“I know.”

“I may not remember why I should come.”

Reed held the edge of her sleeve. She wanted to change her mind. Up above, the frightened town was already preparing for a morning it could understand.

Sera waited until Reed released the cloth. Then she put the crown in place.

## 12. The work after [return-morning]

Reed woke with her cheek against Sera's knee. She had been laid on the hall floor because nobody had been willing to move her farther.

Someone had taken off her shoes. She found herself worrying about losing the borrowed one, then laughing weakly at the size of the worry. Sera put a cup against her hand.

“A little. Sit up first.”

Across the room, people were speaking in unfamiliar tones. A woman was telling the same small story three times. Each time, the man beside her asked to hear it again. Marta was counting the signatures on the roll. She kept reaching the end, checking one mark and starting over.

Reed tried to say that she needed a blanket. Instead she said a name she had never heard. The room slanted. Sera put the cup down and held her until it steadied.

“One of the unclaimed,” Marta said from the table. She came over slowly, carrying her chair. “Tell me what you can see.”

Reed described an orchard in winter. Nobody recognised it. Marta wrote UNKNOWN beside the account and left room for a correction.

“What if it stays?” Reed asked.

“Then we'll make room for it without calling it yours.”

It was not enough. Reed was grateful that Marta did not try to make it sound enough. She pulled the blanket round her shoulders and asked for the cup again.

Outside, people were testing the road with the long rope. Nobody had yet crossed. Reed could see the patience taking effort. Hessa stood by the door, stopping one person after another and telling them the same instruction. The crown lay on the floor with its roots grey and still.

At noon they carried it into the courtyard. Reed watched from the steps. Someone proposed burning it. Someone else wanted it locked away. Reed asked them to leave it in the rain until they had slept before making another permanent decision.

For once, everyone agreed.

## 12. Four steps [open-ending]

Three days later, they took down the rope across the north road.

Reed went with Sera, Marta and Pip. Hessa carried a measuring pole. They had sent no animal first; the old ward had never held animals, and testing it that way would have told them nothing.

The white stones were still there. Reed stepped beyond them. One. Two. On the third, her breath caught because she expected it to stop. It did not.

She took a fourth step and stood there crying, annoyed that the others could see.

Beyond the hill, there were more hills. The old siege road had collapsed into a watercourse. Opening a boundary had not repaired a bridge, found trading partners or taught anyone what a two-hundred-year-old coin would buy.

They returned for spades.

In the weeks that followed, some people remembered more than they wanted. Sera had to go alone to a grave she had forgotten visiting. Marta found that being able to remember signing the destruction orders made her account clearer and harder to give. Nobody asked her to stop giving it.

Reed kept brief scraps of the lives she had carried. She could not always tell whether a remembered orchard belonged to her childhood or someone else's. Sera helped her label the uncertain ones without inventing answers.

## 12. An ordinary examination [empty-morning]

The surgeon asked Reed to follow his finger with her eyes. She did. He asked whether she knew where she was. A woman beside her chair answered too quickly, then put her hand over her mouth.

“I was asking her,” the surgeon said.

Reed looked round. A table, grain bins, a door standing open onto daylight. She could name each thing. She could not explain why everybody was so tired.

“A hall,” she said.

“That's right.” He wrote something down. “Does anything hurt?”

She showed him her calf and her pricked forehead. The injuries were not new. The bandages were clean. Somebody had taken care over her and she could not remember being touched.

The woman beside her chair asked whether she wanted breakfast. Reed said yes because it was the first question whose answer she knew. A boy brought bread. He held the plate with unusual care.

“Do you live here?” she asked him.

He looked towards the woman. “With Aunt Sera. You can too.”

Reed did not know whether that was a promise he could make. The woman seemed to understand.

“For as long as you want a room,” she said. “You don't have to decide anything else.”

Later the surgeon showed Reed a statement signed in handwriting like her own. She read a paragraph, then stopped. The room had become too full of expectation again.

Reed ate another piece of bread. Sera brought more butter. Outside the door, people were calling to one another from a road Reed did not know she had opened.

## 12. The visitor book [vessel-ending]

The road opened. Reed heard the report from people she had met that morning and did not know she had saved.

They brought gifts at first. She found the visits exhausting. Sera wrote visiting hours on the door and sent away three council members who arrived together with a speech. They could leave their gratitude on paper, she said.

Reed learned to work the treadle in the repair shop. Her hands had kept ordinary skills; she could thread a needle, cut bread and follow written instructions. A history read aloud did not give her back the experience of living it.

Sometimes somebody said, “You used to like this,” and waited for her to like it again. Sera began saying, “Ask her now.”

Pip came on the fourth afternoon. He sat on the floor because the good chair was covered in mending. He did not begin with the crown.

“You got tied to a goose,” he said.

She looked up from the hem she was sewing.

“That seems unlikely.”

He explained his reasons. She laughed before he finished. He waited a moment, as if he had heard something through an open window.

“You laughed like that before.”

She did not know what answer he needed. He seemed to realise this and went on explaining the goose.

Pip’s hand had healed crookedly. When Reed asked about it, he told her what had happened at the bridge. She read her own account afterwards and asked whether he wanted to keep visiting. He said he did, but that he might change his mind.

At the new bridge, they put up no statue. Reed had read the proposal and refused. They built a covered bench instead, with a place to leave messages for travellers.

## 12. Tickets for the table [strangers-morning]

By noon, six children were waiting at the public table. The adults there had fed them, but three would not go home with the people who had come to collect them. A written household chart was not enough to make a child take a stranger's hand.

Reed sat at the end of the bench. She could read the destruction statement, including her own name. It did not give her the authority to solve this by telling anyone to be reasonable.

Sera brought paper and began making tickets. The children could stay together for the afternoon. Anyone wanting to visit would come singly and sit across the table. Two people would witness each arrangement. They would try again tomorrow.

One woman said she had spent seven years raising her son and would not ask permission to take him home. The boy put both hands over his ears. Sera waited until the woman saw him.

“He doesn't know that,” she said.

The woman sat down. Reed fetched water. It was all she could think to do.

At the far end of the hall, someone began reading names. People answered when they recognised a fact they had written down. The sound was uneven. Sometimes two answered to the same name. They compared notes instead of choosing the louder voice.

In the courtyard, the white pieces of the crown had softened in the rain. Reed looked at them while she washed the cups. She could not feel the decision as her own, yet her hand fitted the signature on the statement. She went back inside when Sera called for more water.

That evening, they had found rooms for everyone who wanted one. Some slept in houses with people they no longer recognised. Others stayed in the hall. Reed took a blanket and lay beside the door, waking each time somebody needed to pass.

## 12. The names they chose [broken-ending]

They left the doors open for a week, then began closing them again for rain and privacy. Reed found that this ordinary decision was reassuring.

The household charts became necessary in a new way. People read them over breakfast. Some went back to their old families. Others chose separate rooms. At the public table, the rule was that no written marriage or birth gave anyone permission to demand affection from a stranger.

There were arguments about this. There were arguments about almost everything.

Reed worked in the archive copying the destruction statement. She had made that decision for people who had not agreed. The freedom to walk away did not oblige them to thank her. Some did. Some would not share a table with her.

Hessa brought bowls to the repair shop one afternoon. She said the written record showed they had helped each other. She could not remember whether she liked Reed. Reed said she could start with the bowls.

They counted them together. One had a little blue flower in the glaze. Hessa turned it towards the light, then packed it at the bottom of the basket.

Pip asked whether he had to live with Sera. Sera said they could ask the household committee to find another place if he wanted. He thought for two days and stayed. Reed did not ask him why.

In spring, a travelling mason reached the new bridge. They had sent scouts ahead; they knew people lived beyond the basin. Knowing and seeing were different. Reed watched him unpack tools like their own, made in shapes she had never seen.

## 12. The maintenance book [sleep-ending]

The next new moon took two memories from each living person old enough to have them. Children were not charged before their seventh winter. Their parents still stood outside their doors when the muffled bell rang.

Reed slept through it. The first thing she knew of the doubled tithe was a visitor reading from the maintenance book beside her bed. She could hear through the crown sometimes, briefly, without waking enough to answer.

Sera had come to report that the release instructions were carved in six places. The roll was kept under three locks with three different households holding the keys. Oren had been removed from office. He was helping his father learn the new route to the market.

“We've made it harder to hide,” Sera said.

She did not say impossible.

Later, perhaps weeks later, Pip came. He read a description of a boat he was building. His voice changed halfway through. Reed wondered how much time had passed. He had brought a chair and was moving it closer, scraping the floor in short bursts.

“I don't know whether you can hear,” he said. “Aunt Sera says I should come because I want to, not because I owe you.”

Reed tried to move her hand. The crown kept it still.

“I'm angry,” Pip said. “I wanted to tell you while I still remember.”

He stayed for a while after that. She listened to him breathe. When he left, he put the boat drawing beside the bed.

Above them, the mills worked. New houses went up. People taught their apprentices to check the tally twice. On the north road, the white stones were repainted before winter.

In the maintenance book, someone recorded that the guarantor appeared peaceful. A second hand crossed out the word. They wrote instead: Sleeping. We do not know anything else.

**Ending: The Maintenance Book**

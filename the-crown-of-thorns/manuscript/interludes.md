### mill-work | 3 | An apprentice's hands

Before they went to ask questions, Sera sent Reed to the lower mill with a bundle of repaired sacks. Pip went with her. His goose was already there, waiting for the miller to leave his lunch unattended.

“You said he wasn't a pet.”

“He isn't. He doesn't like me.”

Boots followed them through the gate, hissing at Reed's bare ankle.

Inside, a girl was chalking the hopper doors. She had written OPEN over a closed one, rubbed it away and written CHECK WITH TOM. Tom, the miller, waited beside her.

“Do I have to do this every time?” the girl asked.

“Until you know which day it is.”

“I know which day it is.”

“Then why did you put yesterday's flour with today's?”

The girl put down the chalk. Reed expected an argument. Instead Tom opened both bins and checked them with her. They sifted a little onto the same slate and compared the dampness. When they finished, the girl corrected the sack labels herself.

“She lost the week she started here,” Tom told Reed as she untied Sera's bundle. “Can still dress the stones better than I can.”

“I can hear you,” the girl said.

“I meant you to.”

Reed asked why they could not simply use dates from the council. Tom showed her a long beam notched near the ceiling. Each notch was a winter when the river had frozen hard enough to close the wheel. Beneath it was another, finer set of marks, made by generations of millers recording the months between.

“We don't take somebody's word for a count,” he said. “Not even ours.”

The roof beams were older than the apprentice's great-grandmother, whose name Tom read from a page. They had been replaced one at a time. The wheel outside was new; it had been new eleven times, according to the maintenance record. No single person had remembered making all of it, and it had kept turning.

Reed looked at her own hands. She had expected a lost life to be wholly lost. It was frightening to discover how much a person could do while knowing so little about herself.

Tom gave her a barley token for carrying the sacks. He asked her to tell Sera that she had charged too little for the heavy stitching. Pip immediately asked whether that meant she should charge more next time.

“It means you should let a man say thank you without pricing it,” Tom said.

Pip considered that answer all the way out of the yard.

### perimeter | 4 | The boundary by water

Reed walked to the river fence before she went to the granary. A strip of wool had caught on it. Beyond, the river passed between two white stones set on opposite banks, then dropped into a gorge.

A ferryman was repairing the fence. His right foot was missing. He used the bottom of a broken oar as a crutch.

“If you're thinking boats are different, they're not.”

She told him she was thinking about water. The current was leaving. The fish were leaving. A reed stem snapped off upstream could leave.

“Anything but us,” he said. “The third advance takes you, however you're carried. Horse, boat, somebody's back. The boundary counts the motion. We've written it down.”

He showed her a small box fastened inside the fence. It contained copies of accounts. None were tests performed for her benefit. She read of a cart pulled back after its second wheel-turn; of a woman who had been carried out unconscious and died when the men carrying her made their third advance. The exact measure changed with the movement. The warning did not.

“I'm not going to try it,” she said.

“People say that before they come back with an idea.”

“Your foot?”

“Mill accident. Not everything's the curse.”

Reed apologised. He nodded as if she had paid a small, appropriate toll, and went back to the fence.

She stood watching water go where she could not. Her first thought was that someone ought to have found a way. Her second was of the accounts in the box, each an attempt made by a person who had also believed that thought was new.

On the path back she found the remains of an old signpost. Its arms had been turned to point inward. Beneath a layer of moss she could make out the words RETURN TO THE REFUGE. The carving showed the same rooted figure that appeared on the oldest public warning stones.

She made a sketch of it on the wrapping from Sera's sacks. She was beginning to have things she did not want to lose.

### night-watch | 5 | The fourth bowl

That night, before supper, Sera asked Reed to help turn the mattress in the loft. Reed said her leg was well enough. Sera said that was not what she had asked.

They did it slowly, stopping on the middle stair. The mattress smelled of sun-dried rushes. Reed had to squeeze past Sera to keep it from folding into the doorway. It was hard work, and she was pleased with herself afterwards for having done it.

In the loft Sera found an old ribbon under the bed. She put it in her apron pocket without looking at it properly.

“Was it yours?” Reed asked.

Sera brought out a small square of linen. On it she had stitched a name, LENN, and a list of dates. “My husband's. I have the marriage record. I have the day he died. I can't always find him between.”

“Do the dates help?”

“They stop other people telling me it didn't happen.”

She put the linen back. Reed could hear Pip downstairs repeating the sums he had been set. He got to seven threes and went back to six.

“Sometimes I want somebody to tell me,” Sera said. “It seems wasteful to keep a bed for a man whose face I can't remember. Then I hear myself thinking that and I put the extra bowl away again.”

Reed waited. Sera looked embarrassed at having said so much. She took the mattress from Reed's hands and gave her a pillow instead.

“Shake that out. Then we can eat.”

Downstairs, Pip had got to eight threes. He asked Reed what came next. She knew at once. He asked when she had learned it. She did not know.

“That's all right,” he said, after thinking. “You can teach me now.”

They did nine threes together while Sera set the table.

### after-bell | 6 | The people who still came

After the bell, the repair shop opened late. Customers stood outside with bundles in their arms. Sera let them in three at a time and made each one check the name on the ticket before handing anything over.

One man denied that a child's coat was his. It had six different patches and a sleeve turned twice. Sera showed him the payment record. He read it, looked at the coat again and asked whether he had a child.

Pip went upstairs. Reed stayed behind the counter because Sera had asked her to mark collections. She wanted to put down the pen. Instead she wrote the man's name while he and Sera looked for a household witness.

“He's at his sister's,” the witness said when she arrived. “You arranged it. Your son. His name's Benn.”

“Why did I send him away?”

“So he wouldn't watch the bell take you.”

They sat on the steps together until the man was ready to go. He held the coat in his lap as carefully as if the child were inside it.

Reed waited until the shop was empty before asking whether this happened every month.

“Not to everyone,” Sera said. “That's how we keep going. Some of us can still tell the others.”

Outside, a woman began singing a list of grain measures. A second voice joined, then stopped when they reached different numbers. Reed could hear them checking the verse against a board at the market. These were not the empty people she had begun to fear becoming. They were busy, impatient people who had work to do and kept finding new ways to do it.

She went upstairs. Pip was carving the rib of a boat from a split branch. He moved the knife away when she sat down. Reed asked whether he wanted company or quiet.

“Company,” he said. “Quiet company.”

She sat until he asked her to hold the wood. They did not mention the greeting. When Sera called them to the bridge, Pip put the unfinished rib on the sill where the sun could dry it.

### root-room | 7 | A ledger made of wood

Reed did not leave the cellar when the figure withdrew into the root. Oren wanted the door shut. Sera asked him to go upstairs and get clean water for Reed's bleeding leg. It gave him a task he could not turn into a command.

In the silence, Reed heard a woman laughing.

The sound came from the severed branch. She crouched beside it. Under its clear skin were rings like the growth rings of ordinary wood, except that each contained a dark point. On the newly cut face a bead of sap moved towards her hand.

She pulled away before it touched her. The laugh stopped. Somewhere farther along the root, a man began calling a name.

“It's keeping them,” she said.

Sera stood beside her. “That doesn't mean it will give them back.”

Reed asked the empty chair whether the memories were payment or property. The figure returned slowly, assembling itself from the grain of the wood.

“Pledge.”

“Then you haven't spent them.”

“They maintain the separation.”

“That's not what I asked.”

The figure looked down at the severed branch. Its sleeve had no seam. Reed found herself looking for some small ordinary detail that might help her understand how to speak to it.

“The memories of the living remain in pledge,” it said. “The death of an owner closes the account. It does not give that account to another.”

Sera sat on the lowest step. For a while she asked nothing. Reed understood that one of the voices she had hoped to find had just become impossible.

“Your instructions don't say to stop collecting when people can't remember the release,” Reed said.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“They were made by people who could remember it.”

Reed looked towards the cellar window, where a strip of grey daylight showed the underside of someone's passing shoe. The prison had not been designed in one piece. People had added to it, repairing the parts that kept it closed while letting the parts that opened it disappear.

### copies | 8 | What had been corrected

Marta stayed in Sera's kitchen after the others went to look beneath the sluice. Reed asked her to remain until she came back; she needed someone who could compare the chamber's dates with the public account.

When Reed returned, the table was covered in strips of paper. Marta had traced the same entry from four different registers. The earliest said the Crown slept in pledge. The second said the Crown slept to preserve the town. The third said the Crown required the town to remain. In the fourth, a later hand had added the word willingly.

Reed put the scratched message from beneath the bed beside them.

“I agreed to wait for the end of a siege,” she said.

Marta nodded. She did not reach for the paper.

“Did nobody notice?”

“Someone did.” Marta showed her a margin note cut in half by the binding. “They asked which war. A later hand called the question disruptive.”

The final register had been copied during Oren's first year in office. Reed could see why he had believed repairing the chamber was a duty. She could also see every place where somebody had stopped asking.

“Will you say this publicly?” she asked.

Marta laid the four strips side by side. “I said the opposite publicly. There will be people who remember that.”

“There should be.”

Marta looked up. Reed expected her to refuse. Instead the old woman carefully numbered each strip, then made a second set.

When Sera brought tea, Marta asked for hers without sugar. Sera reminded her she had always taken it sweet. Marta considered the cup, tasted it and decided she preferred it this way today. Reed watched the little decision with a concentration that made Marta smile at last.

“You aren't obliged to be who the record says you were,” she told her. “You are obliged to read what you've done.”

Reed folded the message she had written at seventeen. It was smaller than the face of her palm. She had expected proof of her identity to be heavier.

### boat-rib | 10 | Work for two hands

Reed found Pip at the repair-shop bench before the assembly. Sera had left him there with a neighbour while she checked the public tables. The half-made boat rib was clamped in a vice.

“I need the shaving knife,” he said. “The small one.”

Reed found it under a rag. He watched her pick it up. She set it on the bench rather than putting it into his hand.

He showed her where to hold the wood. His drawing specified a curve much shallower than the one he had carved.

“Which one are you trying to make?” she asked.

“The drawing's wrong.”

“Or the wood.”

He examined it, then turned the drawing upside down. That made them both laugh. Reed let him decide which part to change.

They worked until there was a small heap of shavings on the floor. Through the window came the noise of people gathering at the hall. A cart was being loaded with benches. Every time Reed looked towards the sound, Pip told her to hold the rib steady.

“Are you trying to keep me here?” she asked at last.

He put down the knife. “A bit.”

“I have to go.”

“I know.”

He turned the rib over. The curve had begun to fit his drawing. There were several places where he had cut too deeply. He would need another piece of wood, or a different boat.

“Don't let them tell you it's only you,” he said.

Reed sat down on the other side of the bench. The window was open and cold. She had been preparing arguments for the council, for the Keeper, for Oren. She had not prepared an answer for a boy who wanted her to survive being useful.

“I'll ask them to help,” she said.

He nodded, not satisfied, but willing to let her go with that. She swept the shavings into a box before she left. Pip objected that he had been keeping them for something. Reed gave him the box.

### closed-door | 10 | A refusal

The last house on Reed's round belonged to a woman called Eda. She had put a chair across the threshold. Behind it, a man was doing up his coat while an older girl watched from the table.

“My husband,” Eda said before Reed could ask. “He wants to go. I don't.”

The man did not look round. “She said she'd stop me.”

Reed explained that the witnesses could volunteer separately. Eda would not have to hold his share. He could not make her take hers back.

“But it'll all be here,” Eda said. She touched her forehead. “Everything he remembers. He'll want me to remember it too.”

Reed had come to bring people to the hall. She looked at the chair across the door and understood that she was being asked what she meant by willing.

“Your refusal goes in the register,” she said. “So does his agreement. It won't say the household agreed. It'll say who did.”

The girl got up from the table. She asked whether her mother could still live in the house if she refused. Reed said the keeping did not own the house. Eda made a sound that was nearly a laugh.

Her husband took his hat from its peg. He waited while Eda moved the chair. When he had gone outside, she asked Reed what the crown would do with her unclaimed memories.

“I'll have to carry them through the opening.”

“Then you're paying because I won't.”

“Yes.”

Eda put the chair back. Reed could see her wanting a sentence that would make the refusal painless for both of them. Reed could not give her one. She left the copy of the terms and went down the path.

At the gate, the girl called after her. “Will you still open it?”

Reed looked at the hat waiting beyond the hedge.

“I'm going to try.”

### garden-after | 10 | What the memory left out

When Reed withdrew her hand from the crown, Sera was holding a clean cloth. Reed had not noticed her coming back into the room.

“Tell me where you are,” Sera said.

“The hall. Fenmere.”

“What's under your feet?”

Reed looked down. Her shoes were different: one borrowed, one the shoe in which she had woken. “Stone.”

“Good.”

Sera wrapped the finger and waited until Reed could lift the cup she had brought without spilling it. Only then did she ask what had happened.

Reed described the garden. She could remember the bucket handle cutting her palm. There had been a hole in the queen's sleeve, near the elbow. The details seemed absurdly small to have survived all this time.

“Was she kind?” Sera asked.

“Sometimes. I think.”

“You don't have to decide from one morning.”

That was a relief Reed had not known she needed. She had been trying to decide whether a woman could love her daughter and still put her in the chamber. The memory had given her both things. She did not have to throw one away to make the other simpler.

At the table, Marta had left a question on a slate: CAN A NEW CROWN BE REQUIRED? Reed asked it aloud. The Keeper emerged beside the empty chair.

“This pledge names its guarantor,” it said. “It does not appoint another.”

“Not Pip. Not someone born later.”

“No.”

She asked it to write that answer. Sera witnessed the line. Reed wanted the town to have the refusal in a form that could survive somebody needing a different answer next winter.

Outside, two people were arguing about where to put a table. Sera went to help them. Reed stayed a moment longer with the bandage and the written answer. Then she picked up a bench and followed.

### road-preparation | 11 | Before any opening

They agreed that nobody would run for the boundary when the crown broke or the keeping ended. Hessa wrote it at the top of the practical list. They would check the white stones with a rope from inside, then send a person only when Reed said the account was closed.

“And if Reed can't say?” Sera asked.

The room quieted. Hessa turned back to the list.

“Then we wait for the root to die and the bell to stop. We take time. We don't turn her choice into another funeral.”

Reed asked for a word with Sera while the others counted lamps. They went to the little space behind the granary door, where coats were hung over sacks of winter wool.

“If I don't remember,” Reed said, and found that she had prepared nothing after it.

Sera waited.

“Don't make me read everything at once.”

“I won't.”

“If I don't want the name—”

“Then we'll call you what you ask.”

Reed held out the scratched message from the chamber. Sera took it, read it and gave it back.

“That's yours,” she said. “You can keep it or put it away. I'm not going to use it to make you go downstairs.”

For a moment Reed wanted to remain behind the door. There was nothing special about the wool sacks. She could smell lanolin and the onions somebody had stored too close to them. Sera was tired, her eyes red at the edges. Reed could have been an apprentice being told to go home after a long day's work.

Then a bench scraped in the hall. The bell gave a short, muffled sound. Sera touched Reed's sleeve and stepped aside so Reed could open the door herself.

### return-morning | 12 | The work after

Reed woke with her cheek against Sera's knee. She had been laid on the hall floor because nobody had been willing to move her farther.

Someone had taken off her shoes. She found herself worrying about losing the borrowed one, then laughing weakly at the size of the worry. Sera put a cup against her hand.

“A little. Sit up first.”

Across the room, people were speaking in unfamiliar tones. A woman was telling the same small story three times. Each time, the man beside her asked to hear it again. Marta was counting the signatures on the roll. She kept reaching the end, checking one mark and starting over.

Reed tried to say that she needed a blanket. Instead she said a name she had never heard. The room slanted. Sera put the cup down and held her until it steadied.

“One of the unclaimed,” Marta said from the table. She came over slowly, carrying her chair. “Tell me what you can see.”

Reed described an orchard in winter. Nobody recognised it. Marta wrote UNKNOWN beside the account and left room for a correction.

“What if it stays?” Reed asked.

“Then we'll make room for it without calling it yours.”

It was not enough. Reed was grateful that Marta did not try to make it sound enough. She pulled the blanket round her shoulders and asked for the cup again.

Outside, people were testing the road with the long rope. Nobody had yet crossed. Reed could see the patience taking effort. Hessa stood by the door, stopping one person after another and telling them the same instruction. The crown lay on the floor with its roots grey and still.

At noon they carried it into the courtyard. Reed watched from the steps. Someone proposed burning it. Someone else wanted it locked away. Reed asked them to leave it in the rain until they had slept before making another permanent decision.

For once, everyone agreed.

### empty-morning | 12 | An ordinary examination

The surgeon asked Reed to follow his finger with her eyes. She did. He asked whether she knew where she was. A woman beside her chair answered too quickly, then put her hand over her mouth.

“I was asking her,” the surgeon said.

Reed looked round. A table, grain bins, a door standing open onto daylight. She could name each thing. She could not explain why everybody was so tired.

“A hall,” she said.

“That's right.” He wrote something down. “Does anything hurt?”

She showed him her calf and her pricked forehead. The injuries were not new. The bandages were clean. Somebody had taken care over her and she could not remember being touched.

The woman beside her chair asked whether she wanted breakfast. Reed said yes because it was the first question whose answer she knew. A boy brought bread. He held the plate with unusual care.

“Do you live here?” she asked him.

He looked towards the woman. “With Aunt Sera. You can too.”

Reed did not know whether that was a promise he could make. The woman seemed to understand.

“For as long as you want a room,” she said. “You don't have to decide anything else.”

Later the surgeon showed Reed a statement signed in handwriting like her own. She read a paragraph, then stopped. The room had become too full of expectation again.

“Can I finish it tomorrow?”

The boy reached for the page before anyone else could answer. He put it in a clean envelope and wrote TOMORROW on the outside.

Reed ate another piece of bread. Sera brought more butter. Outside the door, people were calling to one another from a road Reed did not know she had opened.

### strangers-morning | 12 | Tickets for the table

By noon, six children were waiting at the public table. The adults there had fed them, but three would not go home with the people who had come to collect them. A written household chart was not enough to make a child take a stranger's hand.

Reed sat at the end of the bench. She could read the destruction statement, including her own name. It did not give her the authority to solve this by telling anyone to be reasonable.

Sera brought paper and began making tickets. The children could stay together for the afternoon. Anyone wanting to visit would come singly and sit across the table. Two people would witness each arrangement. They would try again tomorrow.

One woman said she had spent seven years raising her son and would not ask permission to take him home. The boy put both hands over his ears. Sera waited until the woman saw him.

“He doesn't know that,” she said.

The woman sat down. Reed fetched water. It was all she could think to do.

At the far end of the hall, someone began reading names. People answered when they recognised a fact they had written down. The sound was uneven. Sometimes two answered to the same name. They compared notes instead of choosing the louder voice.

In the courtyard, the white pieces of the crown had softened in the rain. Reed looked at them while she washed the cups. She could not feel the decision as her own, yet her hand fitted the signature on the statement. She went back inside when Sera called for more water.

That evening, they had found rooms for everyone who wanted one. Some slept in houses with people they no longer recognised. Others stayed in the hall. Reed took a blanket and lay beside the door, waking each time somebody needed to pass.

### winter-book | 12 | The first provision

Before she slept, Reed made the assembly say where the extra grain would come from if the doubled tithe left more people unable to work alone. Oren brought the reserve accounts to the chamber door.

“We have enough for winter if the western fields are sown,” he said.

Sera asked who would sow them. Oren began to name the usual households, stopped and read the maintenance plan instead. Two of the named workers were on the well list. One had forgotten which children lived in his house.

They changed the plan. Apprentices would work in larger groups. Older people who could still teach would move nearer the fields. The public table would keep open through the winter. None of it removed the extra taking. It arranged who would carry it.

Reed watched them make the arrangements from the stone bed. She had asked for time; here was the price of having some.

“Don't write that I saved you,” she said.

Marta had been recording the meeting. She looked up, then drew a line through a heading and began another.

Oren set the accounts on the floor. Without the silver clasp, his coat looked much older. He asked if there was anything she wanted taken upstairs.

Reed gave him the public release copy. She had kept the page beside her in case she changed her mind. He recognised what he was being asked to carry and did not reach for it at once.

“Give it to three people,” she said. “Don't choose them yourself.”

He took it to the door. Sera stood aside to let him pass, watching until he reached the light.

Later, when the sleep had begun to settle and Reed could no longer tell whether her eyes were open, she heard Marta reading the revised heading. TERMS OF THIS RENEWAL. Below it she put the date, the names of the witnesses and the warning about the doubled tithe. She read all of it aloud before closing the book.

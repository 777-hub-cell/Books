# The Dragon’s Guest

By NIGHT

A complete reading of the canonical route. The master manuscript and playable edition contain the other choices and two alternate endings.

## 1. The Question of Breakfast

The child had put a collar on the mountain goat.

Ordan lowered his head through the kitchen window, taking a little more stone out of the frame. The goat stood on the table eating his accounts. Linnet stood beside it with an expression he had learned to distrust.

“It followed me,” she said.

“Through three doors?”

“It is determined.”

He drew breath. His daughter pointed a floury finger at his nostril.

“No. We discussed sneezing indoors.”

He shut his mouth. Far below the mountain, seven princes had lately paid to have his sleeping habits established by spies. The spies had disagreed about whether he slept. None had discovered that a girl of eleven could prevent him from sneezing.

“It has eaten your tax assessment,” Linnet said. “You might thank it.”

The goat reached for a second page.

Ordan withdrew from the window. A moment later he came through the door as a tall man in a coat with one sleeve inside out. Changing took nine heartbeats, and left him briefly soft as candle wax; Linnet had been taught to wait until he could speak before throwing herself at him. Today she did not wait. He caught her with a grunt.

“Eleven,” he said. “You are eleven. Your knees have become weapons.”

“Then you should surrender.”

He surrendered. She pushed a heel against his stomach and climbed to his shoulders, as she had done before she could say his name. For years he had carried her in dragon form cupped beneath his throat, terrified of every tiny sound she made. Now she was heavy, opinionated, and convinced that bread needed salt.

She was correct about the bread. He disliked this.

At the cupboard, she leaned down to select a jar. Her dark plait swung across his remaining account books. He had found her beside a road in a basket made for eels, furious at the rain, her mouth open to denounce the weather. He had looked for whoever had left her. There had been no tracks beyond the cart ruts; no village had claimed her, no search had brought an answer. He had returned with a baby and no understanding of how a creature could need feeding so often.

He had learned.

“People in town keep goats,” Linnet said.

“People in town keep them outside.”

“You live outside half the time.”

“I own the mountain.”

She considered this. “So does the goat, probably. Have you asked?”

He felt her laughing through the soles of her feet.

Years later, when he tried to remember the sound, he would remember instead that he had been annoyed about the accounts.

Eight years after the goat, Linnet brought home a brass hinge.

She put it beside Ordan’s plate. He was peeling an apple with a knife too small for his hand. Her boots left wet marks on the kitchen tiles.

“What is this?” he asked.

“A hinge.”

“I had reached that conclusion.”

“For the old storehouse. We’re making a school.”

He set down the apple. She had begun saying we about people who lived in the river towns. He was proud of this in principle and very tired of it in practice.

“The floor floods.”

“Only because the council hasn’t repaired the western sluice.”

“The council has thirty reasons why it cannot repair anything.”

“Thirty-one. They’ve invented another.” She sat beside him and stole a slice. “I’ve done the measurements. The storehouse is sound. Children can come by the high road.”

“And in winter?”

“I intend to let them inside.”

He looked at her. She chewed with deliberate seriousness until he laughed.

The hinge was handsome. Someone had filed its edges smooth. He turned it over and saw her name scratched on the underside, a declaration that she had made something.

“You need not spend your life making them like you,” he said.

“I don’t. There’s a boy who bites. I dislike him.”

“Then why teach him?”

“He still ought to read.”

She took the knife and peeled the rest of the apple in one long strip. Outside, the little summerstone he had given her kept a covered bed of seedlings warm. Such stones held dragon heat for months; they gave it back steadily, no matter who owned them. Linnet had asked for hers to grow cabbages. Kings had asked for theirs to warm siege furnaces. He had refused the kings.

“Don’t go down tomorrow,” he said. “The river smells wrong.”

“That’s why I have to. They’re opening the relief gates without warning the tenants.”

He had arguments ready. She reached across and put the apple peel over his wrist like a bracelet.

“Come on market day. You can frighten the biting boy.”

“I am excellent at that.”

“At biting?”

“At being frightening.”

She kissed his cheek, quick and careless. He thought he would have another opportunity to tell her about the river.

## 2. The Woman in the Ditch

One hundred and twelve years later, Edda Venn lay in a drainage ditch while a cooper’s son tried to break her measuring hand.

He stamped on her wrist. She dragged it beneath her chest and tasted mud. Someone laughed at the sound she made. Her leather survey roll lay open on the road. The brass dividers were gone. So was the little tin of salve she carried for her bad knee.

“Tell them what you did,” said the cooper’s son.

She knew him. Pate. He had once asked her to set a bone in his dog’s paw. She had managed it, though she was a sluice surveyor and her mother had been the bone-setter. The dog had recovered. Pate had never paid.

“I measured a wall,” she said.

He kicked her in the ribs.

The wall belonged to the council. Behind it ran the water that should have reached the lower holdings. Edda had found a private diversion feeding the chairman’s bleaching works, and had put the measurement in writing. By noon she had been accused of opening the floodgate herself. By dusk somebody had painted a dragon’s mouth on her door.

Her brother Sef had gone to the north ferry three days earlier to arrange a berth for them. She had told him she would finish one small job. He was going to be unbearable about it, if she reached him.

The men on the road moved back.

A stranger stood above the ditch. His black coat was old, with beautifully darned elbows. His hair had gone silver at the temples. He looked at Pate’s raised boot and then at Edda’s hand, tucked under her body.

“Continue,” he said.

Pate lowered his foot.

“She opened the water,” someone offered.

“Did she.”

The stranger’s voice was quiet. Edda could see his hands. The nails were wrong: too smooth, too dark, as if someone had polished horn to resemble a man’s nails. Heat dried the mud at the hem of his coat.

One of the men ran. The others followed him. Pate backed away last, still looking for a way to make retreat appear his decision.

The stranger crouched. His gaze caught on the red welts around Edda’s wrist. For a moment he seemed to be seeing something beyond her, and the ditch filled with the smell of hot stone.

She could ask him to help her reach the ferry. Or she could ask him to witness the broken wall: his face had been enough to scatter five men, and she still had a fact worth saving.

“Look at the wall,” Edda said.

The stranger glanced towards the ferry. “You are hurt.”

“I’ll still be hurt in ten minutes. The water may cover it by then.”

He helped her along the ditch. At the breach she knelt, trying not to breathe against her ribs, and brushed silt from the old stone. A brass maker’s plate sat below the new cut. Her fingertips found a raised bird and the letters L.I.N.

“There,” she said. “The old channel points south. They’ve cut through it into the works. You can see the new tool marks.”

The stranger did not answer. He had put his hand flat on the bird.

“Do you know the mason?”

He shook his head once. She had the unpleasant impression that another movement might break something in him.

She took an impression with charcoal and damp paper from her roll. The bird emerged crookedly, followed by the line where old stone ended and fresh work began. She added the angle of flow. Nothing here would prove who had struck her, but it might prove what she had measured.

“A witness should sign,” she said.

“No court would accept my name.”

“They might ask why.”

He looked at her then. “You place too much faith in questions.”

“It’s measurements I trust.”

He went away without signing. Edda dried the rubbing against a warm stone and hid it inside the splint around her wrist. By then the ferry office had shut. She spent the night under the high bridge, watching lanterns pass above.

At dawn the wardens found her. One carried her measuring rod, repaired badly enough that she could see the join.

“Evidence,” he said.

She almost laughed. It would have hurt too much.

## 3. The Fourth Winter

There were twenty-seven red stitches on the inside of the ceremonial veil.

“One for each peace,” said the seamstress.

Edda sat with her hands bound loosely in front of her. They had cleaned the blood from her mouth, but a loose tooth worried her tongue. The seamstress was trying to fit the veil without touching the bruise on her cheek.

“One for each person,” Edda said.

The needle stopped.

In Harrowmere, every fourth winter was a peace winter. The market hung red cloth. Bakers sold little winged loaves. Names were drawn in the council chamber from debt rolls no one outside the chamber could inspect. A selected household received a remission of taxes and a pension. Families sometimes called the payment an honour, because calling it anything else did not feed the remaining children.

Edda’s hearing had lasted less than an hour. Chairman Voss had explained that criminal debts could also be remitted through public service. Flood sabotage carried a debt beyond any ordinary payment. She had asked to inspect the channel with an independent mason. Voss had called this a request for delay.

There had been no drawing.

“My brother,” she said. “Has anyone told him?”

“Your household will receive the allowance.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

The seamstress set the veil down. Her nails were stained blue from cheap dye.

“My mother did this before me. I have two girls.”

Edda watched her thread another needle. She wanted to break something fragile enough to break. The seamstress’s daughters were at school across the lane, learning to write a king’s name. None of this made the woman innocent. None of it would help Edda escape.

“Then put in my name,” she said. “When you make the stitch. Not peace. Edda Venn.”

The woman looked at the door before bending to the cloth.

An hour later the procession climbed towards the mountain. Wardens supported Edda between them so that no one in the town would see her limp. People threw rosemary. One elderly man turned his back. A child asked whether the dragon liked cakes, and his mother covered his mouth.

At the old gate the wardens untied Edda’s hands. They put her inside and closed the iron bars behind her.

She heard them hurry away before the official farewell was finished.

## 4. What He Had Been Going to Do

The dragon waited beyond the courtyard.

Edda had imagined a shape like a horse made enormous. His head alone was longer than the ferry office. Dark scales overlapped along his throat, with pale scars between them. One horn had broken long ago and grown back crooked. He lifted his face from the ground.

She knew the eyes.

“You,” she said.

His jaw shut. Smoke leaked between his teeth and dwindled.

Behind him stood a smaller door, ordinary enough to be ridiculous. It had a brass hinge shaped like a bird. Beyond its threshold she could see a table and two chairs. One chair was covered with a cloth.

She understood what he had been waiting to do. There was no feast laid out, no guard, no apparatus of judgment. He had been lying here as a butcher might wait for someone to deliver an animal.

“What happens now?” she asked.

He did not move.

“You know I didn’t open that gate.”

“I know they hurt you.”

His voice filled the courtyard without becoming loud. It pressed against her ribs. He looked away from the veil.

“Go inside.”

“Why?”

“There is water. A bed.”

“For how long?”

He dragged one claw through the courtyard moss. She had waited through a hearing, a dressing, a procession, and each polite delay had carried her closer to death. She would not mistake delay for mercy again.

“Are you going to kill me?”

The question seemed to cost him effort to hear.

“I was,” he said.

Wind moved the veil against her mouth. The dragon kept looking at the ground. At last he shifted aside, leaving the kitchen door clear.

Edda could demand that he open the outer gate immediately, even though she could barely walk. Or she could accept the bed for tonight and insist on an answer when she could stand without shaking.

“Open it,” she said. “The gate behind me.”

The dragon lifted his head. For a moment she thought he would refuse. Then he reached past her, carefully avoiding the wet red cloth, and drew the bar from its brackets.

The gate swung outward.

There was the path. There were the trees. Beyond them lay the town that had sent her here. She could not walk down the mountain tonight. The fact that the way was open mattered anyway.

“You may stay until you can travel,” he said.

“And after?”

“You may travel.”

“Say I’m free.”

His eyes returned to hers. “You are free.”

She made him leave the bar on the ground.

Inside the kitchen, she found a jug of water and a folded cloth. The covered chair was smaller than the other, repaired at one arm. She chose a stool. The dragon watched from outside until she pulled a curtain over the window.

For the first time that day, no one could see her.

She cried with her hand pressed to her sore ribs. When the crying stopped, she drank. Then she searched the cupboards for something she could use as a weapon and found a rolling pin.

It would not stop a dragon. It fitted her hand. She slept with it beneath the blanket.

## 5. Nine Heartbeats

He changed on the third morning.

Edda was sitting outside with her leg stretched on a bench. The kitchen contained flour but no bread. She had asked him to fetch yeast and he had stared at her until she understood that no one had asked him to fetch groceries in a very long time.

“Watch,” he said. “If you are staying near me, you must know when to move away.”

The scales along his breast rose. Under them light showed like heat through cracked pottery. His body folded inward without becoming smaller all at once. For a few seconds Edda could not have said what stood before her. The joints were unfinished; the left side of his face was a patch of dark membrane that shivered in the cold.

She counted nine beats of her pulse. The man from the ditch stood in the grass, breathing hard.

“I cannot burn while changing,” he said. “The heat is holding the shape. Nor does scale protect what has not become scale.”

“Why show me?”

“Because a startled person may reach for a knife.”

“You think I startle neatly.”

He looked at the rolling pin beside her. She left it there.

He brought yeast from a cold store cut into the mountain. Beside it sat a thumb-sized red stone that radiated gentle warmth. There were larger ones behind iron grilles. Summerstones, he called them. He charged them with his own heat. Ordinary bodies could be warmed by them, but they could not create life, heal a wound or store a soul.

“That one keeps the nursery bed warm,” he said.

“There’s a nursery?”

“For cabbages.”

He seemed to expect laughter. She gave him a small amount, cautiously.

While the dough rose, she cleaned the cuts on her hand. Her mother had taught her to boil cloth and water, recognise swelling, leave a wound open enough to drain. Much of bone-setting involved persuading frightened people to keep still. Edda had preferred surveying because a wall did not argue about how much it hurt.

Ordan watched her bind her wrist.

“Does that help?” he asked.

“Some. Time does the rest.”

He touched his own hand, examining the knuckles as though they belonged to someone else.

## 6. Things Left Within Reach

In the rain, the house made small sounds around her.

A gutter dripped against a bucket. The window latch clicked. Ordan had gone to the far side of the mountain to hunt; he had warned her because she had asked where meat came from and disliked being told only that it would be provided.

Her knee was better. She could walk as far as the gate without stopping. Beneath the kitchen window lay an overgrown cabbage bed. She pulled weeds until the roots came free with satisfying little pops.

On the shelf above the hearth stood an exercise book. Edda opened it to a page headed Difficult Words. Whoever had written it had included millennium, privy, and forgiveness. Beneath forgiveness, in another hand, someone had added: Most frequently demanded by people who owe you money.

She smiled before she could stop herself.

Between the pages lay a drawing of a dragon with his head in an oven. Steam issued from the oven door. A caption read Father has taken charge of the roast.

Edda shut the book. She had no right to be opening everything she found because she was frightened.

The covered chair stood near the wall. She could ask him about the person who had lived here, and risk giving him a question he might refuse. Or she could use the afternoon to inspect the barred store beyond the cold room, where she had seen old council seals among the gold. Her own warrant had borne one of those seals. The answers there might help her get home.

“Your daughter?” Edda asked, when he returned.

Ordan set a bundle of dry branches beside the hearth. One rolled away. He watched it until it stopped.

“Linnet.”

“I opened her book. I’m sorry.”

“She put it there to be read.”

He sat in the larger chair. It creaked. Edda took the stool again.

“She was nineteen when she died,” he said. “She would have objected to people mentioning that first.”

“Then tell me something else.”

He considered. Rain pattered against the glass.

“She disliked plums. Not the taste. The stones offended her. She believed a fruit should make up its mind about being edible.”

Edda laughed. His face moved towards the sound, uncertainly.

He told her about the goat. He did a poor imitation of Linnet’s voice, then stopped and did it again without trying to make it young. She had wanted a school. She had argued about bedtimes with the solemnity of an advocate. When she was small she had put blankets over his claws on cold nights.

“Were they cold?”

“No.”

“Did you tell her?”

“I did not wish to hurt her feelings.”

She could imagine him lying absolutely still while a child struggled with enormous feet. The thought reached her before she remembered the red stitches. Her smile faded.

He saw it.

“I did those things too,” he said.

“I know.”

There was no comfortable answer after that. She did not offer one. Yet when he rose to leave she asked him to put the exercise book back where it had been, uncovered.

He did, with one hand on the shelf for longer than was necessary.

## 7. The Service Bell

On the ninth day the road below the gate filled with men carrying covered packs.

Edda saw them from the kitchen. A woman led them, broad-shouldered, with a faded red cord tied around her wrist. She raised an empty hand when Ordan crossed the courtyard.

“Roof repairs,” she called. “The council sent us.”

Edda had been on council jobs. They sent no one to repair a roof in winter without first sending three people to dispute who should pay. She reached for the little brass bell by the hearth. Ordan had told her it summoned him if she needed help; he had put it there before going to sleep outside, and had asked permission to leave it within reach.

She rang it.

He turned towards the kitchen. The woman shouted, “Now. Before he’s done digesting.”

One of the men pulled a crossbow from his pack.

Ordan was already changing. He had chosen the smaller form to come through the kitchen door. Edda saw the light break between his scales, the unfinished membrane of his cheek. The crossbow bolt struck the wall. A second man, closer than she had realised, drove a narrow climbing spike into his left eye.

The dragon’s head struck stone. Heat burst outward. The attacker fell with his sleeve burning, and the woman dragged him back by the collar.

“The stone,” she shouted. “Take the stone!”

Two men ran for the cold store. Ordan’s hind claws dug furrows through the courtyard. He could not raise his head. Half-made flesh along his jaw shook violently, each attempt to change drawing the spike deeper.

The woman saw Edda in the doorway. “You’re alive.”

For the first time she sounded afraid.

They had come expecting a corpse and a heavy meal. Edda could run through the pantry to the rear chimney and ring the alarm gong, driving the thieves away from the stones. Or she could crawl to Ordan immediately and try to stop him moving against the iron.

Either way, they had seconds.

Edda crawled along the wall, keeping beneath the sweep of his foreleg.

“Ordan. Stop changing.”

He made a sound that shook dust from the lintel. The spike moved. Blood ran hot over the courtyard stones, steaming in the rain.

She had once held a miller’s arm while her mother cut away a shirt caught in a pulley. The miller had screamed that she was hurting him. Her mother had said yes, she was. Edda had remembered that more than any comforting thing anyone had said in the room.

“You’re driving it deeper,” she told him. “You have to stay this size.”

His one visible eye found her.

She put both hands against his jaw. He could have crushed her by turning his head. He did not turn it. Under her palms, the trembling began to slow.

Behind her a grille slammed. Men grunted beneath a weight. The woman in command swore at someone to leave the gold.

“I have to pull it out,” Edda said. “I can’t if you move.”

She wrapped a rag around the spike so she could grip it. Her damaged wrist failed once. She used the other hand and leaned her body away.

The iron came free with a sickening little release.

Ordan’s head dropped. She scrambled clear. He breathed across the stones, a ragged gust so hot the blood dried on her skirt.

The thieves were gone. From the cold store came a wavering red glow: one of the great summerstones had been levered from its cradle. Its broken retaining ring lay on the floor.

Edda pressed a folded cloth over the ruined eye. He held still for her.

## 8. A Smaller Body

“I have to change,” he said near dusk.

Edda sat beside his head. Her skirt had dried stiff. She had washed the wound with water cooled from boiling, though he had flinched from cold far less than she expected.

“It tore you worse last time.”

“It was unfinished. My blood keeps trying to finish it.”

He explained in pieces, between long silences. Dragon flesh carried heat through a network beneath the scales. An injury of this size bled faster than he could close it. A human body would have a smaller wound and less surface to lose warmth. The change itself could kill him if he did it while shaking.

“And the eye?”

“It will be gone.”

He said it flatly. She was grateful for that. There was enough to do without pretending.

She moved the bench cushions into the hearth alcove, fetched the small summerstone and wrapped it in thick cloth. It would warm him while his own heat was occupied. Then she checked the cold store. The damaged great stone would need a new cradle soon. Until then, she closed the fire doors around it, using a lever because the handle was too hot.

“What if I fall?” he asked when she returned.

“Then you fall on cushions. I’ve organised it.”

For a moment the corner of his mouth moved.

She told him to follow the rhythm of her tapping against the floor. Nine measured beats. A surveyor knew how to count while frightened; rivers did not pause to let her recover her nerve.

At the seventh beat his body buckled. She kept tapping. At the ninth, a man lay on the cushions, bleeding from a wound that crossed his brow into the empty socket.

She did not pack the socket or attempt her mother’s work with a blade. She supported his head, covered the eye lightly, and made him drink when he could swallow. For the next three days her tasks were plain: clean water, warmth, changed cloth, listening to his breathing, persuading him that rest did not mean he had stopped defending himself.

His own body closed the torn flesh. The summerstone only kept him warm. Edda could see the difference each time he woke and the wound looked a little less raw.

On the fourth morning he said, “You could have gone.”

She wrung out a cloth. “Yes.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“I knew how to help.”

He waited. She gave him no larger answer.

## 9. The Woman Who Came Back

The captain returned alone with a white cloth tied to a stick.

Ordan was asleep. Edda met her outside the gate, carrying the climbing spike. The woman looked at it and lowered the stick.

“Merrow Sill,” she said.

Edda remembered the red cord. Sacrifice families received them, one for every household member left alive. The dye was supposed to fade before the next peace winter. This one had faded almost to brown.

“You came to rob him.”

“We were paid to take a great stone. Voss wants heat for the works.”

“And you believed we’d be dead.”

Merrow looked through the gate at the kitchen door. “The stories say he eats first, sleeps after. We watched the procession. We thought we had an hour.”

“They’re wrong.”

“I gathered.”

She had brought Edda’s dividers, recovered from one of the men who had attacked her in the ditch. Pate had sold them at a drinking house. Merrow set them on the gatepost. Then she put down a folded contract bearing Voss’s seal.

“Why bring that?”

“He sent wardens to my lodging this morning. Apparently theft is a serious matter now.”

Practical, then. Edda preferred it to a speech about remorse.

Merrow said her mother had come up the mountain eight years earlier. She had bought the family’s seed on council credit after a flood. When the debt was called in, the council offered a remission. Merrow had been sixteen. She had watched the procession from a roof.

“Does he remember her?”

Edda could not promise that he did. Merrow nodded as if she had expected nothing else.

She could hand Merrow the receipts, ask her to help recover evidence in town, and accept that a woman who had attacked them might still be useful. Or she could keep the captain outside their lives and send her to warn the lower holdings about Voss herself.

Edda made Merrow wait while she fetched the leather receipt case.

She had found the case behind the cold-room grille the morning after the attack, looking for spare cloth. The nameless acknowledgments had made her feel sick. Then she had found Mara Sill’s thumbprint.

Mara Sill’s thumbprint was still there.

Merrow took the page with both hands. She read it twice. Her lips moved on the second reading, though she made no sound.

“I won’t give you his life for it,” Edda said.

Merrow looked up.

“And I won’t give him yours. If you work with us, that’s the condition.”

“Us.”

“For this work. Don’t make more of it.”

Merrow folded the receipt. “What work?”

The council kept two kinds of record: judgments in the public chamber, payments in the weighing house. Edda had entered measurements in both. The stamps in Ordan’s receipt case matched those on a century of sluice accounts. If the sacrifice levy paid for flood relief, as Voss claimed, the accounts ought to show where the relief went.

“You want to audit them?” Merrow asked.

“I want to know why they repaired my rod before exhibiting it.”

Merrow gave a brief, unwilling laugh.

She would meet Edda at the abandoned storehouse when Edda could travel. She knew a door into the weighing house. Edda knew what to look for once they were through it.

At the gate Merrow stopped. “I don’t forgive him.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

When Edda brought the contract indoors, Ordan was awake. He read Merrow’s surname, and did not ask why Edda had let her leave.

## 10. Twenty-Seven

Ordan brought the receipts to the table the next morning.

He read every name that remained. Six early names, then scattered later ones. Most pages held only a seal. Twenty-seven people had climbed the path; twenty-seven had died. Nothing in the receipts said how many people had been lost when he burned the river towns before the bargain. He could name the towns. He could not count the dead.

Linnet had been accused of opening the western relief gates to punish tenants who refused the dragon’s protection. They had tried her in her absence while she was locked beneath the weighing house. Witnesses had been threatened with losing their holdings. Her drawings had been called invasion maps. The council had executed her before sending him the verdict.

“They showed me a seal,” he said. “They thought I would accept their authority.”

His voice had grown very controlled. Edda recognised the effort: the tone of someone carrying a full bowl through a crowd.

He had burned the council chamber and the men inside it. The fire had spread to the market. An army had come from the next kingdom. He had destroyed that too. Then the surviving towns offered a bargain: one life every fourth winter, drawn at first from the households responsible for Linnet’s judgment, in exchange for no further attacks.

“Why agree?” Edda asked.

“It gave them a day to fear. I wanted them to know when.”

He had also wanted a memorial. Linnet’s name had been cut into the first gate; each offering was supposed to acknowledge what had been done to her. He had accepted that acknowledgment in lives.

“Then they stopped saying her name,” he said. “I let them.”

The gate now bore only the words PEACE THROUGH SERVICE.

Edda looked at the cloth on the little chair.

“Would she have wanted this?”

“No.”

“You knew that.”

“Yes.”

He did not look away. She found that harder to bear than if he had.

At last he told her he had been considering ending the bargain before she came. He had circled the mountain on the last peace winter and thought of refusing the procession. Then he had landed in the courtyard and done what he had done before.

“Thinking was easier,” he said.

Edda could demand a public refusal now, risking retaliation from the council but giving the next selected family a chance. Or she could insist he first release the written charter into her keeping, so the terms could be challenged in court. Neither action could return a single person to the table.

Ordan wrote the refusal himself.

His handwriting was angular and cramped. Edda watched him cross out the phrase henceforth suspended and write ended in its place.

“No one may offer a person in my name,” he read. “No one owes me a death. I will not accept another.”

“And if they send someone anyway?”

“I will open the gate.”

He signed it Ordan, without the titles kings had given him when they were afraid.

Edda made three copies. She paid a charcoal burner to take one to the lower holdings, one to the ferry, and one to the council. Ordan tried to offer gold. She made him count out ordinary silver instead. The man needed a wage he could spend without explaining how he had come by a royal goblet.

That afternoon someone lit the peace beacon below the town. It was the signal for a breached bargain.

Edda looked at Ordan. “That was quick.”

“Voss has no need to think.”

His attempt at humour was poor. She appreciated the effort anyway.

They watched the smoke rise. He offered to take her anywhere beyond Harrowmere once he could change safely. She told him he was still bleeding through his bandage and that she had a brother to find.

“Then I will carry your luggage,” he said.

“You can start with that bucket.”

He fetched it. The announcement had not made the kitchen easier to heat or the wound easier to clean. Work remained, and for once he did it without needing an enemy.

## 11. Leave-Taking

Edda left on the twenty-third day.

Her wrist could bear the survey roll. Her knee could manage the descent with a stick. Ordan had packed bread, cheese, a small summerstone and so much money she could barely lift the purse.

She removed most of the money.

“It’s yours,” he said.

“Then I can leave it here.”

He watched her tie the bag. His bandage was clean; he could change now, though it left him exhausted. The empty socket had closed beneath a new scar. She had stopped looking away from it. He had begun noticing when she did not.

At the gate he offered her a horse.

“Do you have one?”

“I could obtain one.”

“Please don’t.”

He inclined his head. There was almost a smile.

She had slept in his house, argued with him, washed his blood from her skirt. None of those things meant she owed him a return. She said so because she needed him to hear it, and because some part of her needed to hear herself say it.

“You owe me nothing,” he said.

“Even if I never come back.”

“Even then.”

He stayed inside the gate while she walked down. At the first turn she looked back. He had not followed her.

She kept going.

At the abandoned storehouse, Sef stood beneath the sagging porch, older than he had looked three weeks earlier. His hair stuck up where he had been pulling at it.

He had found her name on the allowance roll. He had sold his tools to buy a mule, then searched every turn of the mountain road.

He hugged her so hard she swore.

“You are,” he said into her hair, “an absolute idiot.”

“I missed you too.”

He held her at arm’s length and examined the scar on her cheek. Then he held her again, more carefully.

For the next seven nights she slept somewhere Ordan could not see. Each morning she could decide what to do with the day.

## 12. Two Sets of Books

The storehouse still had Linnet’s hinge.

Edda recognised the bird on its underside when the wind pushed the door shut. Inside, damp exercise slates leaned against the wall. No school had opened here. The council had made it a store for salt, then abandoned it when the roof began to leak.

Sef wanted to leave the district. She wanted that too, in the way she wanted to pull a thorn from her foot. But her name remained on a conviction. If she crossed into another borough, the warrant could follow her. So could the next procession, with someone else under the veil.

They needed evidence other people could use.

Merrow could get Edda into the weighing house. A contract clerk named Oswin would be there, and he knew the modern payments. The weighing-house route risked discovery, but offered a chance to secure the sealed accounts.

The other path followed the old western channel to the village survey chest, where copies of pre-flood drawings had been deposited outside council control. That would take longer and leave the weighing-house records unguarded. A current rubbing of the diversion could make the old plans evidence of continuing fraud. Without one, they would establish the older crime, but not the modern payments.

Sef set out three cups. “I’m going wherever you aren’t.”

“That’s not a plan.”

“It’s half of one. We have two people.”

“You can’t read the old units.”

“I can carry a chest.”

She put her head on his shoulder for one tired moment. He smelled of wool and river tar. Then she drew the two routes on a slate and told him what not to touch if a floor beam had rotted.

Merrow opened a side door with a key she declined to explain.

Edda followed her into the weighing house. The place smelled of damp sacks and ink. At the long desk Oswin sat with a coal pan under his chair, sorting warrants into bundles. He looked up and went white.

“I was told—”

“People keep saying that,” Edda said.

He glanced at Merrow’s knife. Merrow turned the sheathed blade behind her hip.

Edda laid the theft contract beside her conviction. Same seal. Same clerk’s countersignature. She asked him to show her the flood relief payments.

“Those are private accounts.”

“Public water.”

He stared at her. Then he opened the lower drawer.

There were payments to the bleaching works from a fund named Peace Maintenance. The levy did not support the lower sluices. It paid wardens, ceremonial costs and credit advances to selected households, with interest accumulating until the next peace winter. It also paid Merrow’s company to take a great summerstone.

Oswin’s hands shook so badly he spilled sand across a fresh entry.

“The old judgments,” Edda said.

“Cellar.”

Below, the walls held a century of flood stains. Linnet’s trial lay above the highest mark. A request for witnesses had been filed after the warrant of execution. Edda checked the dates twice. The council had asked for testimony from a dead defendant.

Edda copied the dates and gate instructions into her survey book. These were enough to understand the old accusation; they would not carry the weight of a sealed original.

In the next room boots struck the stairs. Oswin had come down after them.

“They’re burning the upper accounts,” he whispered. “Voss knows.”

Smoke slipped through the joists. Edda could take the sealed payment ledger and leave by the coal hatch. Or she could go back for Oswin, whose cough was already growing thick, and trust Merrow to salvage what she could.

## 13. A Man with a Cough

Edda went back for Oswin.

He had fallen on the stairs. She got beneath his arm and half-carried him towards the coal hatch. Her knee nearly folded at the last step. Merrow reached through, caught his collar and pulled while Edda pushed.

Behind them something heavy fell. The payment ledger lay on the table under a burning beam.

“Leave it,” Edda said.

Merrow looked at her once. Then she dragged Oswin outside.

The clerk coughed until he brought up black phlegm. Edda knelt beside him, furious at how relieved she was to see it. Breathing badly was still breathing.

Merrow had saved three loose pages. None bore the intact seal. They could tell a magistrate what had happened, but Voss could call them forgeries. Oswin would have to testify in person, and he was shaking so badly he could not hold a cup.

At the storehouse Edda told him that. She offered him the north road if he wanted it.

“You’d let me go?” he asked.

“I’m trying to grow used to the idea.”

He slept for fourteen hours. When he woke, he asked for a pen. The first line of his statement took most of a page because his hand would not steady.

Edda fetched more paper.

## 14. The Work She Had Done

Linnet had been right about the gates.

The trial exhibits Edda had copied in the cellar made the sequence plain.

The council had ordered the relief leaves shut so the upper warehouses could keep loading grain. Lower tenants had drowned. Linnet had measured the flood line, copied the order, and prepared to take both to the borough assize.

They had arrested her carrying the drawings. Then they had called the drawings proof that she knew how to flood the valley.

Edda sat at the storehouse table fitting the dates together. No missing conversation would have saved Linnet. The men who signed her death warrant had already seen the warning. They needed her silent before the circuit magistrate arrived. They had called witnesses only after the execution to make the file look complete.

The accusation had served three purposes: conceal the gate order, clear the drowned holdings for council purchase, and turn a dragon’s daughter into a threat from which the council claimed to protect the town.

Her father’s revenge had made the third lie easier to sell.

Sef read the reconstructed sequence and sat down heavily.

“Does knowing help?” he asked.

“It helps me know what happened.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

“I know.”

Outside, someone knocked. Ordan stood under the porch, in human form, with a basket on his arm.

He had not come for her. He had brought the original sacrifice charter and the receipts, and asked whether they could be copied where people could inspect them. Edda had left him a message at the mountain asking for the documents. He had brought every paper, including his own acknowledgments of delivery.

In the basket, beneath the leather cases, he had put a loaf that had risen badly on one side.

Sef examined it. “You made this?”

“Apparently.”

“Ambitious.”

For a moment Edda wanted to laugh and weep with equal force.

## 15. What Sef Wanted

Sef took Ordan outside.

Edda could hear their voices through the warped boards, too low to make out most of the words. She did not go to the door. Her brother had a right to speak to the person who had intended to kill her without being supervised.

When they came back, Ordan was carrying the empty basket. Sef’s eyes were red.

“He says he won’t take you away,” Sef said.

“He can’t take me anywhere.”

“You know what I mean.”

She did. For twenty-three days Sef had imagined her dead, or not dead yet. She could not ask him to put that down because she had begun to think of the kitchen as somewhere she missed.

Ordan waited by the door. “He asked if I would burn the town.”

“And?”

“I said no.”

“He asked what happens if the town hurts you again.” Sef looked at Edda. “He took longer with that.”

Ordan set the basket on the floor. “I should have answered sooner.”

Edda watched him. He could have made a promise too quickly, to please her. Instead he stood looking ashamed of the time it had taken.

“You have to find something to do with being afraid,” she said. “Something besides making other people more afraid.”

“Yes.”

Sef leaned against the table. He wanted her north with him. He also wanted the wardens off their backs and the allowance rolls opened. He had spent days talking to families outside the council chamber. She had thought he was merely waiting for her. He had been learning the work too.

“I can stay here,” he said. “Get people together. You can take her drawings back to him.”

Edda could return to the mountain as a collaborator, with rooms of her own and the road kept open. Or she could remain with Sef through the first petitions, keeping the relationship with Ordan at a distance. The documents and the campaign would continue either way.

She arrived at dusk with her own key.

Ordan had left it at the storehouse, threaded onto a piece of ordinary string. It opened the kitchen and the side gate. He had not attached a message.

Inside, a new chair stood beside the table. It was too large and very ugly. One leg had been shortened twice.

“I misjudged,” he said.

She sat in it. The seat held.

“This is mine?”

“If you want it.”

She put her survey roll on the table. “I want a room with a lock.”

“There is one.”

“A lock from the inside.”

He showed her. She tried it while he stood in the passage. Then she opened the door again.

That evening they ate bread and onion soup. She told him what the drawings proved. He listened without interrupting, one hand flat on the table. When she reached the forged gate instruction, his fingers curled.

“You may be angry,” she said.

“I am.”

“I meant with her too. She went down after you warned her.”

He stared at her. For a moment she thought she had lost the right to sit here.

Then he said, very quietly, “I was.”

He had been angry that Linnet had gone, that she had trusted the road, that she had not called for him. He had taken that anger everywhere except to the person who could not answer it.

Edda let him say it. She did not tell him Linnet would forgive him. She had known Linnet through a book and a few stories, and would not borrow the dead woman’s mouth.

Later, he asked if she was comfortable. She shifted in the dreadful chair.

“Absolutely not.”

He laughed properly for the first time.

## 16. No Last Words

The grave lay behind the old lock keeper’s house.

Ordan had buried Linnet himself. In the years after, he had allowed brambles to cover the place, then told himself he did not remember the path. Edda followed him through the cleared thorns. His coat caught twice. He stopped to free it each time rather than tearing through.

The marker bore only her name and a small bird.

“I thought there should be more,” he said.

“Did you know what?”

“No.”

They stood beside it. Water sounded beyond the wall. Edda had brought the brass hinge from the storehouse, with Bess’s permission. They laid it against the marker, wrapped so the rain would not reach the iron pin.

Ordan told her the last thing Linnet had said to him. She had asked him to move the nursery pots before the frost. He had been irritated at being reminded, because he remembered every year.

“Did you move them?” Edda asked.

“Yes.”

She touched the wet grass. There had been no farewell worthy of a ballad, no final blessing that could tell the survivors what to do. There had been pots to move. Someone had moved them.

He sat on the low wall, and after a while she sat beside him.

“I wanted them to say her name,” he said. “And then I stopped saying the names of the people I killed.”

Edda watched a beetle struggle over a clod. She turned the clod with one finger.

“You could start.”

“It would not be enough.”

“I know.”

He had seven names now, including Mara Sill. Sef’s petitioners had brought more. Ordan said each one aloud, slowly, using the pronunciations Edda corrected. The missing names remained missing. They would have to look for them.

When they left, he looked back at the grave without needing her to wait for him.

## 17. The Hand She Wanted

Spring arrived while they were arguing about a bridge.

Ordan said the span had held for three hundred years. Edda said that was an observation about the past. He said the foundations were deep. She showed him where water had scoured beneath them.

He knelt in the mud to look.

She had spent a month expecting him to give way because he wanted her pleased. This time he asked for the rod, took his own measurement, found he was wrong and swore in a language that sounded like a collapsing quarry.

Edda laughed until her ribs ached with it. He looked up at her from the mud. There was a smear across his cheek. She wanted to put her hand there.

The wanting was specific enough to frighten her.

It had not arrived while she changed his bandages. It had come later: in the way he listened when Sef spoke, the morning he sent money for a widow’s roof without putting his name on it, the hours they spent disagreeing over things that could be measured. He could still become silent with anger. He had learned to say he was angry before leaving the room.

None of it cancelled twenty-seven deaths. She had stopped trying to make affection produce a verdict.

He stood, returning the rod. Their hands touched. He let go first.

“Would you,” he began, and then stopped.

“What?”

He looked at the muddy bank. “Walk with me. After we finish.”

She could say that she wanted more than walks, and ask whether he did too. She could also keep the affection they had built without offering romance. He had not earned a claim on either answer.

They finished the measurements before saying anything else.

Edda found this so funny she almost lost courage. At the top of the bank she put the rod down and said, “I want you to kiss me. If that is something you want.”

He looked at her for a long time.

“I do.”

“All right.”

He did not move. She could see him rehearsing reasons he should refuse on her behalf.

“Ask me what worries you,” she said.

“What if you are grateful?”

“I’m grateful to Bess. I don’t want to kiss Bess.”

His mouth twitched. Then it grew serious again. “I was going to kill you.”

“I remember. I need you to remember without using it to decide what I’m allowed to want.”

He nodded, once. She stepped closer.

Their first kiss was awkward because he turned his blind side towards a low branch and struck it. She laughed against his mouth. He swore, quietly this time. Then he asked if he could try again.

She said yes.

Later, walking back with his hand warm in hers, she told him she was not Linnet.

He stopped. “I know.”

“I need it said.”

“She hated measuring. She put it off until she could no longer avoid it. You measure furniture while people are talking.”

“Only badly made furniture.”

“All my furniture.”

She squeezed his hand. He looked at her, with all the grief still in him and something else making room beside it.

That night she slept in her own room. In the morning he knocked before entering, carrying two cups and no expectation that a kiss had settled the future.

## 18. A Ring of Iron

The great summerstone was no longer safe.

A damaged retaining ring expanded in the day’s warmth and closed badly at night. Ordan drew its shape for Edda, showing where the thieves’ pry bar had broken it. The stone held months of his heat, compressed into a red mass the size of a grain barrel. If the ring failed, it would empty at once. In the cold store, the mountain could contain it. On a cart in a crowded town it would burn through everything around it.

“Can you discharge it?” she asked.

“Slowly. Over a week.”

He could also take the heat back into himself. With his injured heat channels, that might kill him. He explained it before she could ask. No charm or prayer could make the exchange safe; the energy had to go somewhere.

The thieves had taken the great stone. Sef had traced the heavy cart to Voss’s bleaching works. There had already been two fires. Voss refused to empty the works because doing so would admit how the stone had been obtained.

Either way, the theft contract gave Voss a reason to seize what he called council property. The charter had given him a reason to seize Edda. He posted guards at the lower bridges and announced an emergency peace assembly.

All households on the allowance roll were required to attend.

Sef sent the proclamation up the mountain with a question beneath it: How many people can fit in the works yard?

Edda read it and began drawing the yard from memory.

## 19. What the Town Could Hear

They met in the empty storehouse: Edda, Sef, Bess, and the people willing to put their names beneath a petition.

Ordan stood outside until Mara Sill’s sister told him to come in. She did not offer him a chair. He accepted that.

Sef had brought the magistrate’s runner from the neighbouring borough. The assize could issue a preservation order if presented with evidence it could register before Voss convened his assembly. A sealed account book would suffice. So would independent old drawings matched to current measurements. Oswin’s testimony could support an order, but would have to be taken in person before the runner left.

Without one of those, they still had enough to warn people and open the roads. They did not have authority to seize the council stores. Voss would call an armed arrival by Ordan an attack, and some frightened people would believe him.

The room argued about money first. Who would pay pensions when the levy stopped? Ordan offered the ceremonial gold, to be administered by trustees chosen by the families. Bess asked if that bought him pardon.

“No,” he said.

“Then write that too.”

He did.

Edda spread the plans across the table. The summerstone was the immediate danger.

They needed to clear the works yard and take the great stone into the river channel before the cracked ring failed.

The runner waited with his register open. Edda had three possible courses, and could see the costs of each.

The public-hearing course required registered evidence: a sealed book, a living clerk, or old plans corroborated by the fresh channel rubbing. A future in the same house required something else: an affection both had chosen, and enough trust built through difficult acts to sustain it. That course would hold them here through the hearings. Without those foundations, Edda could still put the records before a court later, from the refuge.

She could instead open the north road, establish a refuge beyond the council’s reach, and rescue the people Voss meant to confine. It would cost the chance to hold him here today, but it would give frightened families somewhere to go.

Or she could take Ordan straight to the works before the crowd arrived, relying on his body to contain the stone and the wardens’ violence. His wound made that a grave risk. He said so himself. She could not ask him to do it without hearing that he might die.

The runner dipped his pen. Edda chose what they would carry into the morning.

## 20. The Register Opens

The borough magistrate arrived in a hired cart with one wheel painted blue.

Edda had expected an army after all the trouble of obtaining an order. The magistrate had brought a registrar, two bailiffs and sandwiches. He introduced himself as Vale, asked where the privy was, and then made everyone leave the council chamber until the windows could be opened.

“Records are for reading,” he told the wardens. “That requires light.”

Voss greeted him with controlled courtesy. The chairman was a careful dresser, with a silver chain that held his spectacles. Edda had last seen him from the defendant’s bench. He did not look at the scar on her cheek.

“You have permitted a convicted saboteur to summon an assize,” he said.

Vale opened his register. “I have permitted an allegation of falsified public records to be entered. Her conviction is one of the records.”

People filled the windows outside. Sef had asked the allowance families to come with their papers. Some held them above their heads to keep them out of the rain.

Edda laid out what she had saved. Evidence had boundaries: the old plans proved the alteration that condemned Linnet; modern accounts or testimony showed how the levy had been diverted. Where her proof ended, she said it ended. Voss tried twice to turn an uncertainty into the destruction of the whole case. Vale asked him to answer the part that was documented.

Then Ordan entered.

The chamber fell so quiet Edda heard someone outside cough. He had chosen human form and brought no sword. That did little to make him harmless. Everyone knew what stood beneath the black coat.

Vale asked his name for the register.

“Ordan.”

“Do you claim jurisdiction over these people?”

“No.”

“Do you claim the charter as permission to kill?”

“No.”

Edda saw Voss’s hand tighten on his spectacles. The chairman had expected either a monster threatening the court or a lover pleading for mercy. Ordan gave him neither.

He said what he had done. He did not ask Linnet’s death to stand as his defence.

Outside, a woman shouted Mara Sill’s name. Ordan stopped until she had finished. Then he said it back, correctly.

## 21. What Was Entered

Vale asked Edda whether she wished her own conviction heard first.

It would be quicker. Her survey, Voss’s theft contract and the evidence from the channel made a clear enough case for quashing the warrant pending a proper hearing. Once her name was cleared, she could travel, earn, rent a house without fearing an arrest at the door.

The allowance families waited outside. Their pensions could be suspended the moment the council’s charter was declared void. Voss had already begun calling the payments voluntary generosity. If the hearings broke for the day before those claims were registered, he might move the money.

Edda could put her own freedom first. She had earned the right to want it. Or she could ask Vale to secure the families’ claims before attending to her conviction, accepting another few weeks with her name under a charge.

Ordan stood behind her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him. He did not tell her which mattered more.

“The allowances first,” Edda said. “Before the accounts move.”

Sef closed his eyes. She knew what he wanted to say. Then he went to bring the families inside.

They came one household at a time. A boy carried his grandmother’s papers because her fingers could no longer unfold them. A man had only a strip of red cord and the testimony of two neighbours. Vale entered the claims as claims, neither dismissing the poor evidence nor pretending it was complete.

By late afternoon the bailiffs held the pension accounts under seal. Voss objected that ending the charter ended the obligation. Vale asked which law permitted a public body to purchase a person’s death on credit and then repudiate the debt.

There was no answer the registrar could enter.

Edda’s conviction remained until the next sitting. She took home a temporary protection order and a frustration so sharp she could taste it. Sef walked beside her without speaking for half the road.

“You can be angry,” she said.

“I am.”

She heard Ordan’s answer from the storehouse in her brother’s voice, and was unexpectedly moved by how differently the two men carried it.

“I wanted the paper for you today,” Sef said.

“So did I.”

He put his arm around her shoulders. They walked home slowly. Behind them the accounts stayed where they were, and fourteen families slept with receipts that could no longer be quietly destroyed.

## 22. The Work at the River

Voss’s last order reached the works before the bailiffs did.

He had told the foreman to prepare the great summerstone for inspection, whether the works possessed it or the mountain did. It was to be carried to the yard in its original iron cradle. A public display would, he wrote, establish lawful ownership.

The foreman read the warning Edda had sent and refused. Voss dismissed him. His replacement ordered the men to harness the cart.

By the time Edda reached the lower bridge, smoke came from an empty wheelbarrow left too near the works furnace. The small stolen stones were already there.

The great stone’s broken ring glowed behind the loading gate. It would need moving first.

Ordan looked at the yard. Then he looked at the crowd behind them.

“I could clear it in a moment,” he said.

She heard what a moment could mean.

“We have people who can open the doors.”

He stood with his hands closed while Sef and the dismissed foreman took the keys from the bailiff. It was the longest Edda had ever seen him wait.

The workers came out in pairs. One turned back for a coat, and Ordan made a terrible sound in his throat. Edda touched his wrist.

“He’s coming,” she said.

He came.

They moved the hot stones on sledges packed with river clay. Ordan walked beside each one in human form, drawing off enough heat to keep the damaged iron from failing, stopping before his scarred channels began to shake. There were pauses, arguments, a broken rope. Edda kept the water moving through the empty relief channel. Her knee hurt. Nobody transformed the work into something noble while it was being done.

At dusk the last stone rested below the flood mark, cooling gradually into running water. Ordan sat on the bank too tired to speak. Edda sat beside him with her boots in the mud.

He offered his hand. She took it.

## 23. An Agreement with an End

The hearings lasted through the summer.

Voss lost his office. His conviction came later, for the modern diversion, hired theft and destruction of records. No living court could sentence the men who had killed Linnet; they had died long before the present case. Their titles were removed from the storehouse lintel after the village voted to remove them. Their names stayed in the record of what had happened.

Ordan’s case was harder. No prison could hold him. No human sentence could measure what he had done without pretending the court could enforce it. Vale refused to write a judgment he could not make real.

The families proposed terms instead. Ordan would fund repairs and pensions from a public trust, open the mountain records, grant a right of passage across his land and appear each year to answer claims. The agreement had a fixed term for administration, with review and appeal; it bound no child to inherit another person’s debt. Anyone could decline his money without losing the right to testify against him.

He signed.

Some called that justice. Others called it the best arrangement fear would permit. Edda knew people who held each view and ate at both tables. She would not make a wedding invitation into a test of forgiveness.

She and Ordan argued about the wedding anyway.

He offered the mountain hall. She said the roof leaked. He offered to repair it. She said she wanted the storehouse, where the school would finally open. He said that would mean inviting the biting boy’s descendants.

“They’ve had time to improve,” she said.

He considered. “I have been given the same opportunity.”

They married before the winter rains, in a room with repaired windows and no thrones. Sef cried, denied it, and then cried again when Ordan shook his hand. Bess brought a plum cake. Ordan looked at the stones in the fruit and began laughing so helplessly Edda had to take the plate from him.

After the guests left, they sat on the school steps. He told her why the plums were funny. She had heard the story before. She let him tell it again.

## 24. A House with Two Doors

Their house stood below the mountain, where Edda could reach the works without a day’s climb.

It had two doors because she insisted on a second way out of every room that might flood. Ordan said she had built an entire dwelling as an objection to the council. She told him the objection was well founded.

They kept separate workrooms. They quarrelled about boots, money and his habit of agreeing to repairs before learning what they would require. Sometimes he woke believing the procession was at the gate. Sometimes she woke with mud in her mouth that was only memory. There were mornings when neither could be useful to the other. They learned to make breakfast anyway.

When they began speaking of children, they did it in daylight over the kitchen table. Neither knew what dragon inheritance would mean in a human child. Ordan knew that his human form was living flesh, capable of fatherhood; he had never chosen it before. They sought a midwife willing to say what she did not know. They agreed that fear was reason to prepare, and that neither of them would use a child to repair a grave.

Their daughter was born four years after the hearings. She came early, furious at the cold. Ordan stood outside until Edda shouted his name with such conviction that the midwife went to fetch him personally.

The baby’s hands were warm. As she grew, she sensed the buried summerstones before anyone showed her where they lay. She could hold heat that made other children let go. A fever could still hurt her. A knife could cut her. She had inherited neither her father’s centuries nor a right to rule anyone.

They named her Teren. Linnet’s name belonged to Linnet. They took Teren to her grave when she was old enough to walk the path, and told her about the goat.

Teren was the beginning of the line, not the beginning of a kingdom. Generations later, descendants of that household would use their inherited command of heat, their endurance and the loyalty built around them to conquer neighbouring territories. The dynasty would become powerful; under its rule a kingdom would know long prosperity. Its chronicles would favour victories over the work of floodgates, and legitimate marriages over other branches of the same blood. Those omissions would matter to a prince and to a girl who did not know her ancestry, in a story far beyond Edda’s life.

Here, there was only a small child who had found a snail on the kitchen step.

Ordan crouched beside her. His blind side turned towards Edda, who quietly moved the hot pot out of his way.

“Can it stay?” Teren asked.

He looked at the snail. He looked at Edda.

“Outside,” he said firmly.

Their daughter began her argument.
